Week 3 — Organization: Structure and Culture

This is individual work. You rate the organization yourself. The comparison at the end is only worth something if the first set of numbers is genuinely yours.

Chapter 3 says culture decides whether a project structure works. This studio makes you act on that claim: rate a real organization on all ten dimensions, work out what structure its culture would force on a demanding project, and then find out what a model does with the same task.

Part 1 · Rate it yourself — 20 minutes

Pick one organization you can actually observe. In order of preference:

  1. A current or former employer, or a place you interned.
  2. A student organization, team or lab you have been part of long enough to have seen it handle a disagreement.
  3. NDSU, or your college within it — treat students as the employees and faculty as the management, exactly as the chapter’s own exercise does.

Pick the one you have the most direct evidence about. You are going to be asked for that evidence.

Now score all ten dimensions on a 1 to 5 scale, where 1 is the left pole and 5 is the right pole. The supportive range column is the band from Figure 3.7 — the positions that suit complex project work. Do not look at it while you score. Cover it, score the ten, then uncover it.

# Dimension 1 = 5 = Supportive Your score Your evidence
1 Member identity Job Organization 4–5
2 Team emphasis Individual Group 4–5
3 Management focus Task People 3–4
4 Unit integration Independent Interdependent 4–5
5 Control Loose Tight 2–3
6 Risk tolerance Low High 4–5
7 Reward criteria Performance Other 1–3
8 Conflict tolerance Low High 4–5
9 Means-ends orientation Means Ends 2–4
10 Open-system focus Internal External 4–5

The evidence column is not optional and it is where most of the marks are. One concrete observation per row — something you saw, not something you assume. “Nobody outside the team knew the launch had slipped for three weeks” is evidence for a low conflict-tolerance score. “The culture is quite closed” is not evidence, it is the score written twice.

Where you genuinely cannot produce an observation, write “no evidence” and leave the score blank. A blank row you can defend is worth more than a number you invented, and you will want the contrast later.

Part 2 · Overlay, and name the gap — 10 minutes

Now uncover the supportive column and compare.

  1. Mark every row where your score falls outside the supportive range.
  2. Pick the two rows furthest outside it. These are the two dimensions on which this organization is least hospitable to project work.
  3. For each of the two, write one sentence on what specifically would go wrong for a project manager here. Not “communication would suffer” — “a project manager would not hear about a slip until the milestone was already missed, because raising a problem early is treated as complaining”.

Then the structural conclusion, in two or three sentences:

Given these ten scores, a large cross-disciplinary project in this organization would need [functional / weak matrix / balanced matrix / strong matrix / dedicated team], because …

Use the argument from LO 3-7. A culture already sitting in the supportive band supplies focus, integration and cooperation for free, so a light structure will do. A culture well outside it does not, so the project has to be separated out and given its own authority to survive.

Part 3 · Now ask the model — 15 minutes

Open Gemini and paste this exactly as written, filling in your organization’s name. Do not give it your scores, and do not give it the supportive range — you are testing what it produces on its own.

Rate the organizational culture of [ORGANIZATION NAME] on these ten
dimensions. For each, give a score from 1 to 5, where 1 is the first pole
and 5 is the second.

1. Member identity: 1 = identify with their job, 5 = identify with the organization
2. Team emphasis: 1 = individual, 5 = group
3. Management focus: 1 = task, 5 = people
4. Unit integration: 1 = units independent, 5 = units interdependent
5. Control: 1 = loose, 5 = tight
6. Risk tolerance: 1 = low, 5 = high
7. Reward criteria: 1 = performance, 5 = other factors
8. Conflict tolerance: 1 = low, 5 = high
9. Means-ends orientation: 1 = means, 5 = ends
10. Open-system focus: 1 = internal, 5 = external

For every score, state the specific evidence you are basing it on.

Then ask it one follow-up, and keep the answer:

For each of those ten ratings, tell me how you know. Which ones are based
on something you have actually read about this organization, and which are
inferred from organizations of this type?

Record its ten scores next to yours. Keep the transcript link.

If the model declines to rate, saying it has no specific information about your organization — that is a legitimate result and possibly the most interesting one. Do not argue it into guessing. Record the refusal, note which of the two prompts produced it, and write your reconciliation about that instead. Say what the refusal tells you about where the ten dimensions have to come from.

Part 4 · Reconcile — 20 minutes

This is the graded part. Everything before it was raw material. One page, four things:

  1. The biggest disagreement. Name a dimension where the two scores differ by 2 or more. Say which of you is right and why, using your evidence from Part 1. If the model is right and you were wrong, say that.

  2. What it claimed to know that it cannot know. Quote the model. Most models will produce ten confident scores for an organization they have never observed, and attach evidence that turns out to be generic on inspection — true of any firm in the sector, or true of the organization’s public statements about itself rather than its behaviour. Find one and name exactly what is wrong with it. If its second answer admitted which ratings were inferred, quote that instead and say whether the admission matches where it actually went wrong.

  3. Where it changed your mind. At least one row, and what specifically moved you. If it changed your mind nowhere, say so and defend that — but you must have asked the follow-up first.

  4. Does the structure conclusion survive? Feed the model’s ten scores through the Part 2 argument. Does it land on the same structure you did? If it lands somewhere different, that is the whole studio in one line: say what a manager who trusted those numbers would have built, and what would have happened.
