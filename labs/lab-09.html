Week 9 — Scheduling Resources and Costs

This is individual work. Parts 1 and 2 are done by hand, then checked against the notebook. Part 5 brings the model in and tests it against the notebook. What is graded most heavily is Part 5 and the price you put on the gap in Part 3: a schedule is a decision about money, and the reconciliation is where you show whose schedule to trust.

The project is the conference from Weeks 6 and 7. Its network is unchanged, and the $70,000 baseline from Week 7 is now allocated to activities. What is new is that each activity needs one or two event coordinators while it runs, and the team is smaller than the early-start schedule assumes. The Week 9 notes walk through the three-coordinator case and show the two-coordinator result; this studio has you produce both by hand, test the heuristic against the optimum, and then answer the question the notes leave open: what is the cheapest way out?

You work on paper and check yourself in the Week 9 levelling notebook. Open it in Colab, choose File → Save a copy in Drive so your edits are kept, and edit only the cell marked EDIT. Its default data is the table below. If Colab is blocked on your network, download the notebook and upload it to Colab from another connection. If you want to see any schedule as calendar dates, the Smartsheet section of the notes rebuilds Tuesday’s sheet from the Smartsheet import workbook.

The parallel method’s rules, as the chapter states them.
Rule Statement
Logic An activity starts only after every predecessor has finished
Limit On no day may the coordinators in use exceed the number available
No splitting Once an activity starts it runs to completion with the coordinators it was given
Priority When two eligible activities compete, minimum slack first, where slack = LF − today − duration; then shortest duration; then lowest ID

Part 1 · Load the early-start schedule — 15 minutes

By hand first.

  1. Compute ES, EF, LS, LF and total slack for all nine activities. These are the Week 6 values; reconstruct them from the predecessors rather than copying.
  2. Draw the early-start load: for each day 0 to 24, add up the coordinators of the activities in progress. Mark the peak and every day above three.
  3. Apply the chapter’s classification test to a team of three coordinators. Is this project time-constrained or resource-constrained? Write one sentence that names the test and the answer.

Then check. In the notebook’s EDIT cell set LIMIT = 3 and choose Runtime → Run all. Section 1 prints the CPM table and section 2 draws the loading chart. Compare both with yours; correct any difference and note what caused it.

  1. Three coordinators. Level the project and record the start day of every activity and the finish day.
  2. Two coordinators. Repeat with two. Record the finish day.

Check two things before looking at the notebook. No activity started before all its predecessors finished. No day uses more coordinators than exist.

Then compare. Section 3 of the notebook prints the parallel method’s own log. Run it with LIMIT = 3, then with LIMIT = 2. For each, find the first period where your log and the notebook’s differ, if any, and say which is right and why. If you broke the priority rule on purpose somewhere, say so and keep your result; it is evidence for Part 3.

Part 3 · Heuristic versus optimal — 20 minutes

The parallel method is a heuristic. With nine activities there are only 26 orders in which they can start without breaking a predecessor, so the optimum can be found by trying them all. Section 4 of the notebook does exactly that; section 5 checks both schedules; section 6 reports them side by side.

  1. With LIMIT = 2, record the notebook’s optimum finish and start days next to your Part 2 result. The gap between the parallel method and the optimum is the price of the heuristic, in days.
  2. The sponsor’s date is day 32 and the venue charges $1,500 for every working day past it. The summary table converts both schedules into dollars; check one of them by hand.
  3. In two or three sentences, explain why the rule lost. Look at period 2 of your log: what did minimum slack see, and what did it fail to see?
  4. Scenario 3. The department will lend a third coordinator for five consecutive days at $600 a day, days of your choosing. In the EDIT cell keep LIMIT = 2 and set LOAN = (19, 23, 1), meaning one extra coordinator on workdays 19 to 23. Run all and read the summary’s penalty + loan column. Try at least three other windows. Find the window with the lowest total, and compare it with the two-coordinator optimum without a loan and with a third coordinator for the whole project (LIMIT = 3, finish day 27, at $600 a day for every day of the project). Which would you recommend to the sponsor, in one sentence with the numbers?

Part 4 · Time-phase the budget — 15 minutes

Section 6 of the notebook spreads each activity’s cost evenly over its days and totals it by five-day week for the early-start schedule, the parallel method and the optimum.

  1. Run with LIMIT = 3 and with LIMIT = 2, and record the cumulative planned value at the end of each week for every schedule. Check that each totals $70,000.
  2. Say which baseline you would control the project against, and why that one.
  3. Status check. At the end of week 2 the sponsor has been invoiced $52,000. Against which baselines is that over, against which is it not, and what would you need to know before calling it an overrun?

By now you hold three answers for the two-coordinator case: your hand-levelled schedule from Part 2, and the notebook’s parallel-method and optimum schedules from Part 3. Then answer:

  1. Is the model’s schedule feasible? Apply the three checks to it by eye: logic, limit, no splitting. Models often start D before B finishes, use three people on a day while claiming two, or pause E and resume it. Name the day and the activity for each failure you find. To be sure, set LIMIT = 2 and LOAN = None, run all, and type the model’s start days into the notebook’s check function in a new cell: check({"A": 0, "B": ..., ...}, "model").
  2. Which of the three does it match? Your Part 2 schedule, the notebook’s parallel method (day 39), the notebook’s optimum (day 35), or none. If it claims its schedule is the shortest possible, check that claim against the optimum and say whether it is true.
  3. Who is right where they differ? For every line where the model and the notebook disagree, say which is correct and why. The notebook tried every feasible order, so a model schedule shorter than the optimum is infeasible somewhere; find where.
  4. What changed your mind? If the model found something you had wrong in Part 2, correct your log and say so. If the model was wrong and the notebook right, record the error.

Keep the reconciliation to one page. If the model’s schedule is feasible and matches the optimum, say so in the first row and describe the two checks you made to be sure. That is a finding, not a failure.
