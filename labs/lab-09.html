Week 9 — Scheduling Resources and Costs

This is individual work. Parts 1 to 4 are done without AI. Part 5 brings the model in. What is graded most heavily is Part 5 and the price you put on the gap in Part 3: a schedule is a decision about money, and the reconciliation is where you show whose schedule to trust.

The project is the conference from Weeks 6 and 7. Its network is unchanged, and the $70,000 baseline from Week 7 is now allocated to activities. What is new is that each activity needs one or two event coordinators while it runs, and the team is smaller than the early-start schedule assumes. The Week 9 notes walk through the three-coordinator case and show the two-coordinator result; this studio has you produce both by hand, test the heuristic against the optimum, and then answer the question the notes leave open: what is the cheapest way out?

Download the Week 9 resource studio workbook. It has eight sheets: Activities, Load ES, Level 3, Level 2, Loan, Log, Budget and Reconciliation. Fill the yellow cells. The load grids and the budget calculate from the start days you enter.

The parallel method’s rules, as the chapter states them.
Rule Statement
Logic An activity starts only after every predecessor has finished
Limit On no day may the coordinators in use exceed the number available
No splitting Once an activity starts it runs to completion with the coordinators it was given
Priority When two eligible activities compete, minimum slack first, where slack = LF − today − duration; then shortest duration; then lowest ID

Part 1 · Load the early-start schedule — 15 minutes

  1. On the Activities sheet, fill ES, EF, LS, LF and total slack for all nine activities. These are the Week 6 values; reconstruct them from the predecessors rather than copying.
  2. On Load ES, enter each activity’s ES as its start day. Read off the peak load, the days over the limit of three, and the project finish.
  3. Apply the chapter’s classification test to a team of three coordinators. Is this project time-constrained or resource-constrained? Write one sentence that names the test and the answer.

Part 2 · Level by hand — 25 minutes

Work period by period, exactly as the parallel method says. Start a row in the Log sheet for every period in which you had a choice to make: who was eligible, their slack at that moment, and what you started or delayed.

  1. Three coordinators. On Level 3, enter the start days your decisions produce. The Over by row must be empty when you finish. Record the finish day.
  2. Two coordinators. Repeat on Level 2. Record the finish day.

Check two things before moving on. No activity started before all its predecessors finished. No activity’s start day was chosen by anything other than the priority rule; if you broke the rule somewhere, say so in the log and keep the result.

Part 3 · Heuristic versus optimal — 20 minutes

The parallel method is a heuristic. With nine activities there are only 26 orders in which they can start without breaking a predecessor, so the optimum can be found by trying them all. Open Google Colab, paste the code below into a cell and run it.

ACT = {"A": (2, [], 1),       "B": (5, ["A"], 2),      "C": (6, ["A"], 1),
       "D": (4, ["B"], 1),    "E": (8, ["C"], 2),      "F": (5, ["C"], 2),
       "G": (3, ["D", "E"], 1), "H": (4, ["F", "G"], 2), "I": (2, ["H"], 2)}
AVAILABLE = [2] * 60        # coordinators available on each day

import itertools

def schedule(order):
    start, load = {}, [0] * 60
    for a in order:
        dur, pred, need = ACT[a]
        t = max([start[p] + ACT[p][0] for p in pred], default=0)
        while any(load[d] + need > AVAILABLE[d] for d in range(t, t + dur)):
            t += 1
        start[a] = t
        for d in range(t, t + dur):
            load[d] += need
    return start, max(start[a] + ACT[a][0] for a in ACT)

best = None
for order in itertools.permutations(ACT):
    if any(order.index(p) > order.index(a) for a in ACT for p in ACT[a][1]):
        continue
    start, finish = schedule(order)
    if best is None or finish < best[1]:
        best = (start, finish)
print("best finish:", best[1])
print("start days:", best[0])

  2. The sponsor’s date is day 32 and the venue charges $1,500 for every working day past it. Convert both schedules into dollars.
  3. In two or three sentences, explain why the rule lost. Look at day 2 of your log: what did minimum slack see, and what did it fail to see?
  4. Scenario 3. The department will lend a third coordinator for five consecutive days at $600 a day, days of your choosing. On the Loan sheet, change five consecutive cells of the Available row from 2 to 3 and schedule against the new limits. To test a window in Colab, edit the AVAILABLE list the same way. Which would you recommend to the sponsor, in one sentence with the numbers?

Part 4 · Time-phase the budget — 15 minutes

The Budget sheet spreads each activity’s cost evenly over its days and adds up by five-day week for any set of start days you give it.

  1. Enter the start days for early start, late start, your three-coordinator schedule, your two-coordinator schedule, the optimum, and your Scenario 3 schedule. Check that every row totals $70,000.
  2. Read the cumulative row for each. Say which baseline you would control the project against, and why that one.
  3. Answer the status check at the bottom of the sheet: at the end of week 2 the sponsor has been invoiced $52,000. Against which baseline is that over, against which is it not, and what would you need to know before calling it an overrun?

Part 5 · Let the model level it, then argue — 15 minutes

Give Gemini the problem cold, without your schedule. Use your NDSU Gemini Pro account; the free tier can run out partway through a studio. If you cannot get access, see the instructor or TA rather than paying for an account.

In the Reconciliation sheet, compare its schedule with yours and with the optimum, line by line. Models often do one of three things: start an activity before a predecessor finishes, use three coordinators on a day while claiming two, or pause an activity and resume it later. Check for each. For every difference, say who is right and why. Correct your sheet if the model found a real improvement; record its error if it made one.

Keep the reconciliation to one page. If the model’s schedule is correct and matches the optimum, say so in the first row and describe the two checks you made to be sure. That is a finding, not a failure.
