# Module 3
# Success, in more than one dimension

	IMSE 456/656 · Week 1 · Larson Ch. 1

If the iron triangle is the constraint set rather than the scorecard, what is the scorecard?

The most useful answer in the literature comes from Aaron Shenhar and Dov Dvir. Their 2001 paper in *Long Range Planning* proposed four dimensions; the 2007 book *Reinventing Project Management* added a fifth. It is the five-dimension version that most of the empirical work now uses, so it is the one we will use.

The trick of it is the right-hand column. Each dimension is measured on a different clock.

---

# Five dimensions, five clocks

	| Dimension | The question | When you can answer it |
	|---|---|---|
	| Project efficiency | Did we hit time and budget? | Immediately |
	| Impact on the customer | Does it meet requirements — and get used? | Months |
	| Impact on the team | Did the team grow, or burn out? | Months |
	| Business / direct success | Did it produce commercial or mission value? | 1–2 years |
	| Preparing for the future | Did it build capability, technology, position? | 3–5 years |

Row one is the iron triangle. The whole iron triangle, reduced to one row of five.

That is the point of the table. It is not that efficiency does not matter — it is that it is the dimension you can measure soonest, which is not the same as the dimension that matters most.

And notice what that does to incentives. The dimension available on the day the project closes is the dimension you get judged on. The dimensions that would vindicate or condemn you arrive after everyone has moved on. The pavilion project manager was scored "did not meet expectations" on row one, and handed the second pavilion on rows four and five, by the same organization, without anyone noticing the contradiction.

---

# The number everyone quotes

	Bent Flyvbjerg's database, **16,000+ projects**:
	
	**0.5%** — on budget, on time, **and** with the promised benefits
	**8.5%** — on budget and on time
	**47.9%** — on budget alone

From *How Big Things Get Done*, Flyvbjerg and Gardner, 2023; the underlying method is his 2014 paper in *Project Management Journal*.

Half a percent. That number does a lot of work in this field, and you will hear it quoted at you by consultants for the rest of your career.

The category breakdown, mean cost overrun: nuclear waste storage two hundred and thirty-eight percent. Olympic Games one hundred and fifty-seven. Nuclear power one hundred and twenty. IT seventy-three. Buildings sixty-two. Rail thirty-nine, tunnels thirty-seven. Wind thirteen. Solar one.

Look at the bottom of that list before you look at the top. Solar comes in at one percent. Whatever is going wrong at the top of the list is not "projects are hard."

---

# Now immediately complicate it

	**Chapman (2024)**, *Project Management Journal* — Swedish transport infrastructure:
	228 road projects, 96 rail. Typical road overrun **2.7%**; rail a **1.8% underrun**.
	93% of road and 70% of rail projects achieved their intended benefits.
	
	**Chapman (2025)**, *IJPM* — 125 rail + 280 road projects across
	Denmark, Norway and Sweden, 2008–2022: delivered **on cost, over time**.

I want you to sit with the size of that gap.

One body of evidence says megaprojects are catastrophically and near-universally over budget. Another, looking at hundreds of transport projects in three countries over fourteen years, finds typical overruns of a couple of percent, a rail underrun, and most projects delivering the benefits that were promised — with no evidence of the optimism bias and strategic misrepresentation that the first account treats as near-universal.

Both are real data. Both are peer reviewed. They are not measuring the same population, and neither one is a universal constant.

---

# The conclusion to draw

	Base rates are not properties of **projects**.
	They are properties of a **governance regime**.

That is the transferable lesson, and it is worth more to you than either number.

When you hear "seventy percent of IT projects fail," the right response is not agreement and not scepticism. It is four questions. Which projects — what sector, what size, what era? Failed against what — the triangle, or benefits? Who assembled the sample, and did the successes have any reason to report themselves? And compared to what — what is the counterfactual where nobody managed the project at all?

You will be asked to do exactly this in the studio work and in the conceptual assignments. A claim grounded in a primary source, or nothing.

---

# Why the dimensions change the answer

	**Gemino, Horner Reich & Serrador (2021)**, *PMJ* 52(2), n = 477:
	
	Agile and hybrid approaches significantly improved **stakeholder success**
	over traditional — while producing statistically **the same**
	budget, time, scope and quality outcomes.

Read that twice, because it is the cleanest demonstration in the literature of why this module exists.

If you measure only the iron triangle, this study says the delivery approach does not matter. Traditional, agile, hybrid — same numbers. You would conclude the entire agile debate was noise.

If you also measure stakeholder success, the same 477 projects say the approach mattered a great deal.

Nothing about the projects changed between those two conclusions. Only the instrument changed. That is what a measurement artifact is, and once you can see it here you will start seeing it everywhere — including in your own final project, where you get to choose what you report.

---

# What to hold on to

	1. Five dimensions, five different clocks.
	2. The dimension you can measure **soonest** is the one you get **judged** on.
	3. Base rates belong to a **governance regime**, not to projects in general.
	4. Change the instrument and you change the finding.
