# Module 2
# The iron triangle, and how it lies

	IMSE 456/656 · Week 1 · Larson Ch. 1

If you have seen one project management diagram in your life, it is this one. Scope, time, cost. Sometimes quality in the middle, sometimes floating as a fourth leg. Pick two, or the classic version — good, fast, cheap, pick two.

I am going to use it all semester. It is genuinely useful. And I want you to understand, in Week 1, exactly what it does not tell you, because almost every project disaster you will study is a project that satisfied this model or was destroyed by it.

Three failures. In order of how badly they hurt.

---

# Failure 1: it is a constraint model, not a success model

	Meeting all three legs tells you **nothing**
	about whether the output was worth building.

The triangle answers "did we execute the plan?" It cannot answer "was the plan worth executing?" Those are different questions and only one of them determines whether anyone is glad you showed up.

You can hit scope, time and cost perfectly and deliver something nobody uses. That is not a hypothetical — it is the single most common outcome of large internal IT projects, and it scores green on every dial the triangle has.

---

# Sydney Opera House

	1957 estimate: **£3.5 million** (about A$7 million)
	Scheduled completion: **26 January 1963**
	Actually opened: **20 October 1973**
	Final cost: **A$102 million**

Roughly fourteen times the estimate. Ten years late.

By the iron triangle, this is one of the worst-executed construction projects of the twentieth century. And it is not remembered as a failure. It is on the currency. It is a UNESCO World Heritage site. It is the reason a great many people can identify Sydney from a silhouette.

I am not telling you overruns do not matter. They destroyed careers on that project and the architect left the country. I am telling you the triangle produced a verdict — *catastrophic failure* — that history did not ratify, and that a model which can be that wrong about that famous a case deserves to be interrogated rather than memorized.

---

# Failure 2: it hides the asymmetry

	The three legs are **not** symmetric.
	
	Scope is usually the only one anyone will actually flex —
	and the flex is usually **unacknowledged**.

The diagram is drawn as an equilateral triangle, which quietly implies the three are equally negotiable. In practice they are not, and the ranking is almost always the same.

The date is often contractual, regulatory, or attached to a season, an election, or a trade show. The budget was approved by people who will need to be asked again in public. Scope is the one that lives in a document, and documents can be edited quietly.

So scope is what gives. The failure is not that scope flexes — that is often correct. The failure is that it flexes *without anyone recording that it flexed*, and so at the end the project reports on time and on budget, and the customer receives two thirds of what they thought they were getting and does not have the paperwork to say so.

When you build a charter in Week 4 and a change control process later, this is the thing you are building against.

---

# Failure 3: there is no stakeholder in it

	Time. Cost. Scope.
	
	**Whose** time? **Whose** cost? Who decided the scope was right?

Look at the model. There is no person in it.

The person who paid, the person who will use it, the team who built it, and the neighbour who has to live next to it can all read the same finished project and reach different verdicts. The triangle has no room to express that, so it silently adopts one stakeholder's view — usually the sponsor's — and calls it the answer.

You saw this in the pavilion case. Fourteen percent over, three months late. For the market vendors that was a lost season and four of them went out. For the council it was a rounding error against a permanent asset, and they voted six to one to pay for it and then commissioned a second one.

Same three numbers. Opposite verdicts. The triangle cannot hold both, and the disagreement was the entire content of the case.

---

# So why is it on every slide in the industry?

	Because it is the only success model you can put in a **contract**.

This is the honest answer, and it is worth respecting.

Scope, time and cost are the three things that can be written down in advance, agreed by two parties, and adjudicated afterwards without either side arguing about feelings. "Did we deliver the specified scope by the specified date for the specified price" is a question a court can answer.

"Did we produce value" is not. Not in a contract, not in a procurement process, and not in a status meeting on a Tuesday.

So the triangle survives because it is *legible*, not because it is complete. That is a real virtue. Keep it — and stop treating it as the scorecard, because it was only ever the constraint set.

---

# What to hold on to

	The triangle measures **execution against a plan**.
	It does not measure whether the plan was worth executing,
	it hides which leg you actually traded,
	and it does not say **whose** success it is reporting.
