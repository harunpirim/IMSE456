# Module 1
# What counts as a project

	IMSE 456/656 · Week 1 · Larson Ch. 1

Every project management course opens with a definition, and most students skip it because it sounds like vocabulary. It isn't. The definition is a decision, and by the end of this module you will see which decision it is.

Here is the standard one. A project is a temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result.

Two words carry all the weight. Temporary. Unique. Everything else is packaging.

---

# Temporary does not mean short

	**Temporary** = a definite beginning and a definite end.
	Not *brief*. Not *rushed*. **Bounded.**

A twelve-year bridge is temporary. A two-hour software patch is temporary. Temporary means the endeavour is designed to stop, and everyone involved knows roughly when.

Why does that matter? Because a bounded endeavour has an end state you can be wrong about in advance. You can estimate it, commit to it, and be measured against it. An unbounded one cannot be wrong in the same way — it can only be running well or running badly today.

That is the entire reason this discipline exists. Projects are the kind of work you can be publicly wrong about.

---

# Unique does not mean unprecedented

	**Unique** = not previously produced in this exact configuration.

The eleventh house a builder puts up on the same street is still a project. Different lot, different soil, different buyer, different price of lumber that month.

Unique does not mean nobody has done anything like it. It means the specific combination has not been executed before, so your knowledge of how it will go is inference rather than measurement.

That is the source of the uncertainty you will spend the rest of this semester trying to manage. Not ignorance — inference.

---

# Operations are the other thing

	**Operations:** ongoing, repetitive, no designed end.
	Optimize a *known* process.
	
	**Projects:** bounded, unrepeated.
	Execute an *unrepeated* one under uncertainty.

A hospital running its emergency department is operations. That same hospital installing a new records system is a project.

The techniques genuinely differ. Operations management is largely about reducing variance around a process you have run thousands of times — you have data. Project management is about committing to a number you cannot have data for, and then defending or revising that commitment as reality arrives.

If you take the wrong toolkit to the wrong work, you fail in a predictable way: you either over-plan something that just needed running, or you improvise something that needed a plan.

---

# Now the boundary cases

	A **film production** — temporary, unique. But the studio makes one every year.
	A **software release train** — every release is unique, the cadence is operational.
	A **refinery turnaround** — recurring every four years, planned as a project every time.

Take a minute on each of these, because this is where the definition stops being vocabulary.

The film. Each film is unique, and it has a wrap date. But the studio's *portfolio* of films is a repeating process with known variance — that is why studios can forecast a slate and individual producers cannot forecast a film.

The release train. Ship every two weeks, forever. Each release contains unique content. Is that fifty projects a year or one operation? Both answers are defensible, and the teams that pick the wrong one either drown in charters or lose track of what they promised.

The turnaround. A refinery shuts down for maintenance every four years. It is recurring — and every operator on earth plans it as a project, with a schedule, a budget, and a critical path, because the cost of an extra day of downtime is measured in millions.

---

# The definition is a decision

	"Project or operation?" is not a fact about the world.
	
	It is a **management choice about which toolkit to apply** —
	and you can be wrong about it.

That is the sentence I want you to leave this module with.

Nothing in physics tells you the refinery turnaround is a project. The operator decided it was, because treating it as one produces better outcomes than treating it as routine maintenance.

So when you look at work in your final project this semester and ask "is this a project?", the honest form of the question is: *would this work go better if I aimed project machinery at it?* Sometimes the answer is no, and saying so is a legitimate finding.

---

# Two words you will need

	**Program** — a group of related projects managed together,
	because managing them together produces benefits managing them
	separately would not.
	
	**Portfolio** — projects and programs grouped for **strategic** reasons.
	They need not be related at all.

The distinction that matters: a program exists because of *dependencies between the projects*. A portfolio exists because of *competition for the same money*.

If someone tells you they run a program and there are no dependencies between the pieces, they run a portfolio and have chosen a more impressive word.

---

# Why there is more of this work than there used to be

	Product lifecycles shortened.
	Everything is now partly a software project.
	Work crosses organizational boundaries by default.
	Regulation and stakeholder scrutiny both grew.

There is a real structural claim underneath the marketing here, and it is this: the proportion of economic activity that is unrepeated has gone up.

When a firm made the same thing for thirty years, most management was operations management. When a firm's product changes every eighteen months and half its capability is delivered by partners, most management is the management of unrepeated work under contract.

That is the world you are graduating into, and it is why this is a required course rather than an elective.

---

# What to hold on to

	1. **Temporary** = bounded, not brief.
	2. **Unique** = this configuration, not unprecedented.
	3. The classification is a **choice**, and it can be wrong.
