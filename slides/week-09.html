Where we are going

  • Compute the full EVM metric set from PV, EV, AC and interpret each.
  • Select an EAC formula appropriate to the situation and justify the choice.
  • State what EVM cannot detect, and describe Earned Schedule’s fix for the SPI pathology.

The frame

Metric Formula Reading
Cost variance CV = EV − AC < 0 = over cost
Schedule variance SV = EV − PV < 0 = behind (in $, not time)
Cost performance index CPI = EV / AC < 1 = over cost
Schedule performance index SPI = EV / PV < 1 = behind
EAC (typical variance) BAC / CPI Current cost performance continues
EAC (atypical) AC + (BAC − EV) Variance was a one-off
EAC (both indices) AC + (BAC − EV)/(CPI × SPI) Schedule pressure will keep costing money
ETC EAC − AC Remaining spend
VAC BAC − EAC Projected over/under at end
TCPI (BAC − EV)/(BAC − AC) Efficiency required to still hit BAC

Worked example

Project: BAC = $500,000, planned duration 20 weeks. At end of week 8: PV = $200,000, EV = $170,000, AC = $190,000.

  • CV = 170,000 − 190,000 = −$20,000 (over cost)
  • SV = 170,000 − 200,000 = −$30,000 (behind schedule)
  • CPI = 170/190 = 0.895 → getting 89.5 cents of value per dollar
  • SPI = 170/200 = 0.850
  • EAC (typical) = 500,000/0.8947 = $558,824
  • EAC (atypical) = 190,000 + 330,000 = $520,000
  • EAC (both indices) = 190,000 + 330,000/(0.8947 × 0.85) = $623,910
  • ETC (typical) = 558,824 − 190,000 = $368,824
  • VAC = 500,000 − 558,824 = −$58,824
  • TCPI(BAC) = 330,000/310,000 = 1.065 → you now need 6.5% better than planned efficiency for the rest of the project, having averaged 10.5% worse. Ask the class whether that’s plausible. It almost never is, and that is the single most useful thing EVM tells a manager.

The spread between $520k and $624k is the lesson. The formula choice is a judgement about causation, not a computation. Make students defend a choice.

The SPI pathology. As a project approaches completion, EV → BAC and PV → BAC, so SPI → 1 regardless of how late the project is. A project delivering a year late shows SPI = 1.00 on its final day. SPI is also denominated in dollars, not time.

Earned Schedule fixes this. ES = the time at which the planned value equals the current earned value: find t such that PV(t) = EV. Then SPI(t) = ES/AT, and IEAC(t) = PD/SPI(t).

Continuing the example with PV accruing linearly at $25,000/week: ES = 170,000/25,000 = 6.80 weeks. AT = 8. SPI(t) = 6.80/8 = 0.850. IEAC(t) = 20/0.85 = 23.5 weeks — a forecast in time units, which SPI cannot give you. (With a linear PV curve SPI and SPI(t) coincide; with a realistic S-curve they diverge, and they diverge most near the end, which is exactly where you need the number.)

Does EVM actually forecast well? Yes, with caveats, and the evidence is decent. Batselier & Vanhoucke (2015, JCEM) evaluated EVM forecasting on 51 real projects (mostly construction) plus Monte Carlo support and found that highly accurate time and cost forecasts are obtainable, while identifying which methods perform best. Barrientos-Orellana et al. (2021, 2023) compared 27 duration and 30 cost methods across 4,100 simulated networks and found the simplest formulations were most stable and accurate, with accuracy degrading as networks become more parallel. De Marco et al. (2023, 79 projects) report EAC MAPE improving from ~14.6% early to ~11.4% late under a proactive risk strategy.

What EVM cannot see: quality, stakeholder satisfaction, whether the scope was worth building, rework not yet discovered, and any risk that hasn’t yet hit the ledger. A project can show CPI = 1.0 the week before it is cancelled.

Check yourself

  1. CPI = 1.05 and SPI = 0.80. What is probably happening on this project?
  2. Which EAC formula would you use if last month’s overrun was a one-time supplier failure? What if it was a systematic underestimate?
  3. TCPI = 1.4. What are you going to tell the sponsor?
  4. Why does SPI become useless near the end, and what replaces it?

Attack this claim

“EVM gives objective, quantitative project control.”

Attack: EV depends on a subjective percent-complete judgement (0/100, 50/50, and level-of-effort rules all produce different EVs from the same work); the baseline is a forecast, so EVM measures deviation from a guess; it is silent on quality and value; and it can be gamed by claiming credit early.

Studio

Give teams three months of raw PV/EV/AC data with one anomaly seeded (e.g. a month where AC drops because an invoice was late). Teams must produce a forecast and identify the data-quality problem. Most produce the forecast and miss the anomaly — which is the debrief.
