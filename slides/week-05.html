LO 5-1 · Estimates support decisions

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 5

From the WBS to a plan

Week 4 defined work packages that could be estimated, budgeted, resourced and owned.

Week 5 supplies the time, effort and cost.

Without credible estimates, the WBS remains a classification of work rather than a plan.

What estimates make possible

Before approval During delivery
Test whether expected benefits justify the cost Sequence and schedule work
Compare alternatives Plan people, equipment and materials
Forecast funding and cash needs Measure progress against a baseline
Set an authorization boundary Forecast the likely finish

The planning horizon

Constructed from the planning-horizon discussion in Larson & Gray, Ch. 5

An estimate is a decision input

An estimate states what the team expects under defined conditions.

It does not promise that uncertainty has disappeared.

A decision maker needs the number, the evidence, the assumptions and the possible outcomes.

LO 5-2 · Four dimensions of an estimate

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 5

Four dimensions answer four questions

Dimension Question Example
Range What outcomes could occur? 10 to 15 days
Accuracy How close was the estimate to the outcome? Estimated $2,500, actual $2,500
Precision How exact is the statement? 15 days instead of by month-end
Confidence How much trust do we place in it? 90% chance of finishing by Friday

Range states possible outcomes

Wider range Narrower range
10 to 15 days 11 to 13 days
$2,000 to $3,000 $2,400 to $2,600
Signals greater uncertainty Requires stronger evidence

For a low estimate of 4 days and a high estimate of 12 days:

\[ \text{range width}=12-4=8\text{ days} \]

Accuracy compares estimate with outcome

Estimate Outcome Assessment
10 to 15 days 12 days Accurate
10 to 15 days 9 days Inaccurate

Finishing early can still expose an inaccurate estimate when the result sits outside the stated range.

Precision states exactness

Less precise More precise
By the end of the month 15 days
Less than $1,000 $850

A precise estimate can still be wrong.

A less precise range can be more useful when uncertainty remains high.

Confidence states trust

90% chance the work finishes by Friday

Less than 50% chance the work costs under $2,000

The confidence claim must name the outcome and rest on evidence such as comparable work, historical error, simulation or informed judgment.

Responsibility and evidence

  1. Put responsibility near the work.
  2. Use more than one informed view.
  3. State normal operating conditions.
  4. Use consistent units.

Senior review can challenge assumptions. Authority does not replace task knowledge.

Independence, contingency and risk

  1. Estimate each work package independently.
  2. Keep contingency visible.
  3. Add risk assessment before commitment.

Hidden reserves destroy the relationship between a risk and the allowance created for it.

Effort and duration answer different questions

40 labor hours describes effort.

5 working days describes elapsed project time under staffing and calendar assumptions.

Two people do not automatically complete forty hours of effort in two and a half days.

A useful estimate record

Record Evidence
Identity Scope, WBS code, estimator and date
Method Top-down, bottom-up or hybrid
Values Low, likely and high values with units
Conditions Assumptions, exclusions and normal conditions
Risk Drivers, contingency and source of rates

Ratio estimating

If comparable conferences cost about $180 per attendee, a rough estimate for 300 attendees is:

\[ 300\text{ attendees}\times\$180/\text{attendee}=\$54{,}000 \]

The relationship must come from comparable work and use consistent units.

Apportioning a total through the WBS

Function points

Element Low Average High
Inputs 2 3 4
Outputs 3 6 9
Inquiries 2 4 6

The weighted count becomes effort only after it is paired with historical productivity.

Effort does not determine duration

If five function points normally require one person-month:

\[ 500\text{ function points}\div5=100\text{ person-months} \]

The result does not mean that 100 people can finish in one month. Work may be sequential, and coordination grows with team size.

Bottom-up methods start with work packages

Method Basis
Template Adjust a completed, comparable work package
Parametric Multiply a task quantity by a measured rate
Range Record low, likely and high values

Bottom-up estimates require stable detail but support budgeting, scheduling, resources and accountability.

Template estimating

Reuse grows stronger when the prior record contains:

  • scope and conditions;
  • the original estimate and method;
  • actual duration, effort and cost; and
  • changes and realized risks.

Copy the evidence, not the old number alone.

Parametric estimating

\[ 120\text{ screens}\times0.75\text{ labor hours per screen}=90\text{ labor hours} \]

Validate the unit, the rate and the comparability of the work before multiplying.

Range estimating

WBS Work package Low Likely High Width
1.2.1 Select venue 3 d 5 d 8 d 5 d
1.3.1 Configure registration 4 d 6 d 10 d 6 d
1.4.1 Confirm speakers 6 d 10 d 18 d 12 d

The likely value is the most plausible outcome, not automatically the arithmetic midpoint.

Three cost categories

Category Meaning Examples

G&A overhead Supports the organization HR, finance, legal, facilities

Cost Likely classification Reason

LO 5-7 · The outside view

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 5

Why megaproject estimates fail

  • Long horizons multiply unresolved assumptions.
  • Novel interfaces make omissions more likely.
  • Optimism favors a successful internal story.
  • Strategic understatement can improve the chance of approval.
  • Sunk costs make later cancellation politically difficult.

Reference class forecasting

  1. Select a class of comparable completed projects.
  2. Build a distribution of actual outcomes.
  3. Adjust the proposed forecast using that distribution.

The outside view asks what happened to projects like this one.

A reference-class uplift

If comparable tunnels averaged a 34% overrun:

\[ \$100\text{ million}\times1.34=\$134\text{ million} \]

The uplift changes the burden of proof. Advocates need evidence before assuming the current project will outperform its reference class.

LO 5-8 · White elephants

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 5

Signals

  • Benefits have fallen below the original case.
  • Operating and maintenance costs require continuing subsidy.
  • Prestige or political commitment replaces evidence.
  • Sunk cost is used to justify future spending.
  • No credible stop, exit or repurpose rule exists.

The forward-looking decision

With the evidence available today, would we spend the remaining money to obtain the remaining benefit?

Money already spent explains how the project reached the decision point. It does not justify the next dollar.

LO A5-1 · Learning curves

Following the Chapter 5 appendix in Larson & Gray [1]

Repetition changes the estimate

\[ T_n=T_1n^b \]

where \(T_n\) is the time for the \(n\)th unit and \(b=\ln(r)/\ln(2)\) for learning rate \(r\).

With an 80% learning rate and a 10-hour first unit, the second takes 8 hours and the fourth takes 6.4 hours under the unit-time model.

The limit of the curve

Learning curves fit genuinely repeated work under comparable conditions.

They do not justify reducing the estimate for unique design work.

A changed process or technology may reset the curve.

The chain

  1. Define the work.
  2. Choose a method that fits the decision.
  3. State units, range and confidence.
  4. Keep assumptions and contingency visible.
  5. Classify costs consistently.
  6. Return actual results to the estimating database.
  7. Use the outside view for large commitments.
