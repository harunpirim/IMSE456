LO 3-1 · Three ways to organize a project

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 3

Where we are

Week 2 ended with a project selected, ranked and funded.

This week: it now lands inside an organization that already exists and already has other work to do.

What a structure has to settle

A good system appropriately balances the needs of both the parent organization and the project by defining the interface between the project and parent organization in terms of authority, allocation of resources, and eventual integration of project outcomes into mainstream operations.

Larson & Gray, Ch. 3

Three nouns, three arguments

Authority — who can tell the engineer what to work on this week.

Allocation of resources — whose budget the engineer is paid from.

Integration — what happens to the result when the team disperses.

Answer 1 · Inside the functional organization

Create nothing. Hand the project to the hierarchy that already exists.

Segments go to the units that own the expertise. Coordination rides on the management channels already running between them.

Where one function dominates, it gets the job of coordinating the whole.

Functional — the trade

Advantages Disadvantages
No change to the organization The project is nobody’s core work
Flexible use of specialists Poor integration across segments
In-depth expertise Slow — up one function, down another
Easy transition afterwards No ownership of the whole

The same fact from two directions: what makes it cheap is what leaves it unowned.

Answer 2 · A dedicated project team

Pull a team out. Full-time project manager, full-time specialists, its own unit.

Dedicated team — the trade

Advantages Disadvantages
Simple — functions left intact Expensive — duplicates capability
Fast — decisions made inside Internal strife
Cohesive — the project is the job Cut off from functional depth
Cross-functional integration Hard to place people afterwards

Projectitis

The we-they split has a name.

The isolation that produces the team’s cohesion produces its estrangement.

You cannot buy one without risking the other.

Skunkworks

Separation taken to its logical end.

Team removed from the building, released from the organization’s normal procedures — on the theory that the procedures are what is slowing it down.

Answer 3 · The matrix

Refuse to choose. Lay a horizontal project structure over the functional hierarchy and run both.

Read a row and you see a project team. Read a column and you see a department.

The engineer at the intersection belongs to both. That is the design, and the problem.

Matrix — the trade

Advantages Disadvantages
Efficient — resources shared Functional/project conflict
Strong project focus Infighting over scarce people
Easier transition afterwards Two bosses is a real cost
Flexible balance of authority Slow — agreement across peers

LO 3-2 · Three kinds of matrix

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 3

One variable changes the job

Project manager Functional managers
Weak Coordinator — influence, not authority Keep control of work and people
Balanced Defines what Concerned with how
Strong Controls trade-offs and assignments; final say Consulted, but do not direct

The strong matrix, precisely

It “attempts to create the ‘feel’ of a project team within a matrix environment.”

The team never leaves the functional hierarchy. It is made to feel as though it has.

Larson & Gray, Ch. 3

Why balanced is the hardest

The what / how line is cleaner on the page than in a corridor.

Is the choice of database a requirement, or an implementation?

The line exists — but it gets drawn during the argument, not before it.

LO 3-3 · The project management office

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 3

Close to universal

79 percent of U.S. organizations report having one.

Matrix organizations did not arrive fully formed. PMOs are often what carried them from an arrangement on paper into one that delivered.

Cabanis-Brewin, 2022, cited in Larson & Gray, Ch. 3

The boundary

the primary role of a PMO is to facilitate/enable projects, not do projects

Scheduling, planning, budgeting stay with the project manager.

Larson & Gray, Ch. 3

Why the boundary keeps getting breached

It always looks like helpfulness.

The PMO owns the methodology → is asked to review a schedule → asked to fix it → owns it.

Now the project manager is accountable for a plan somebody else wrote.

LO 3-4 · Choosing a structure

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 3

Decided twice

Organization level. How much of what we do arrives as projects?

Project level. How much autonomy does this project need to finish?

The seven factors

  1. Size of project
  2. Strategic importance
  3. Novelty and need for innovation
  4. Need for integration — departments involved
  5. Environmental complexity — external interfaces
  6. Budget and time constraints
  7. Stability of resource requirements

Hobbs & Ménard (1993), as presented in Larson & Gray, Ch. 3

The rule, and its two catches

The higher the levels of these seven factors, the more autonomy and authority the project manager and project team need to be successful.

A direction, not a threshold. No score converts into a structure.

Factor 7 runs the other way. Stable resource needs argue for less autonomy, not more.

LO 3-5 · Why culture belongs here

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 3

Structure is a poor predictor on its own

Boxes and lines say who may instruct whom.

Organizational culture — the shared norms, beliefs, values and assumptions that give members a common understanding of how things are done here.

What culture does

  • Gives members an identity
  • Legitimises how the organization is managed
  • Sets standards of behaviour no document specifies
  • Produces social order

Culture is to an organization roughly what personality is to a person.

The blunt consequence

You will spend the job asking people who do not report to you to do things that are not their first priority.

Whether that is normal or presumptuous is not in the org chart.

LO 3-6 · Reading a culture

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 3

Ten dimensions, each a continuum

Redrawn from Larson & Gray, Ch. 3, Figure 3.5

“Bureaucratic” is three separate claims

Tight on control. Low on risk tolerance. Internal on open-system focus.

Each can be checked. Any of them might be wrong.

Where the evidence comes from

Physical characteristics — space is expensive, and gets spent on what is valued.

Public documents — what the organization wants to be seen valuing.

How people behave — who speaks, how disagreement goes, how fast bad news travels up.

Stories and folklore — who the heroes and villains are.

LO 3-7 · Structure and culture together

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 3

The range that suits project work

Redrawn from Larson & Gray, Ch. 3, Figure 3.7; band positions measured from the source figure

Three rows do not want an extreme

Control — loose, but not at the far end. Slack to absorb surprise; enough control that a schedule means something.

Means-ends — almost dead centre. Care only about results and the methodology is dropped the moment it is inconvenient.

Management focus — only slightly toward people. All task burns the team; all people does not ship.

The payoff

Structure and culture are substitutes.

A dedicated team or strong matrix exists to supply focus, integration and authority.

A culture already in the supportive band supplies all three at no cost.

Which runs both ways

A supportive culture can carry demanding projects on a light structure.

A task-focused, tightly controlled, conflict-averse organization cannot.

There, the project has to be separated out and given its own authority — not because the work is harder, but because the culture will otherwise metabolise it.

The chapter’s last word

Regardless of how effective your strategy or project structure is supposed to be, the culture of your company ultimately determines success.

Read the culture before choosing the structure.

Larson & Gray, Ch. 3
