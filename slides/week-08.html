Chapter 7 · Managing risk

Week 7 notes, LO 7-1 to LO 7-9 and Appendix 7.1

What the risk register needed from the links before it

  • From Ch 6: which activities are critical, where the merges are, how much slack each branch holds. A time buffer goes at the merge and on the near-critical branch.
  • From Ch 5: estimates with their ranges and risk drivers, and contingency held visibly, not padded into tasks.
  • From Ch 4: a scope statement to measure change against, and a change-control path for the contingency plan that fires.

Carry these

  • Four steps, one loop: identify → assess → plan responses → control, with control feeding new risks back.
  • Events, not outcomes. “The conference opens late” is a consequence. “The venue’s legal team signs late” is an event.
  • Five threat responses: mitigate · avoid · transfer · escalate · retain.
  • Response vs contingency plan: a response runs now, inside the baseline; a contingency plan waits for a trigger and is drawn from a reserve.
  • Two reserves: contingency (identified risks, project manager) and management (unidentified risks, senior management).
  • PERT: \(t_e=(a+4m+b)/6\), add variances along the critical path, \(Z=(T_S-T_E)/\sigma\).

The five responses, in one table

Response What it does Example
Mitigate lowers likelihood or impact load-test the registration site a week early
Avoid changes the plan so the event cannot occur drop the livestream, record instead
Transfer moves the financial consequence, at a price cancellation insurance; fixed-price AV contract
Escalate hands it to whoever owns the authority a hiring freeze rumour goes to the sponsor’s executive
Retain accepts it deliberately, in the register a once-a-decade storm on the day

Transfer does not remove the risk. Retention is a decision, not an omission.

Response · contingency plan · trigger · owner

Column Test
Response Scheduled work with a budget line. Happens whether or not the event does.
Contingency plan Outside the baseline. Runs only if. Paid from a reserve.
Trigger Observable. “No travel confirmation 21 days out,” not “the speaker seems unreliable.”
Owner One named person who watches the trigger and can act.

Practice A · multiple choice

Two risks are rated on likelihood, impact and detection difficulty, each 1–5.

Risk L I D RPN
R-A Keynote cancels 2 5 2 20
R-B Coffee runs out 5 2 2 20

Which statement is correct?

  1. The two risks deserve the same response budget, because their RPNs are equal
  2. R-B should be handled first, because it is more likely
  3. The RPN organizes the discussion but does not make the decision; most organizations weigh impact above likelihood, so R-A is the major risk
  4. R-A should be retained, because it is unlikely

Answer

(c). Multiplication cannot tell 2 × 5 × 2 from 5 × 2 × 2. The severity matrix is deliberately asymmetric: the major zone runs further down the high-impact column than along the high-likelihood row. A one-in-ten chance of losing the keynote outranks a near-certain catering annoyance.

The exam will ask you to read a number like this, not only to compute it.

The three PERT errors that cost marks

The exam’s one numerical problem runs PERT on top of the critical path. You will do the same in the Chapter 6 practice. First, the errors.

  1. Adding standard deviations instead of variances. (1 + 2 + 2 = 5, not 3.)
  2. Summing every activity in the network instead of the activities on the critical path.
  3. Running the passes on m instead of \(t_e\). The pessimistic tail is usually longer, so \(t_e > m\) and the project is longer than the most-likely plan.

And one for interpretation: the single-path probability is optimistic. A shorter path with a larger variance can become the longer one at a merge.

Practice C · short answer

The register lists “attendance is low.” Rewrite it as a risk event with a cause and an effect, and give it a trigger.

Chapter 6 · Developing a project schedule

Week 6 notes, LO 6-1 to LO 6-6

What the schedule handed to risk

  • A critical path: the activities with no reserve under the current plan.
  • Merge activities: they wait for the last of several predecessors and inherit every delay. That is where a time buffer earns its keep.
  • Slack by branch, so the team knows which near-critical path could become controlling.

Carry these

  • Forward pass: \(EF = ES + D\); at a merge, \(ES = \max\) of predecessor EFs.
  • Backward pass: \(LS = LF - D\); at a burst, \(LF = \min\) of successor LSs.
  • Total slack \(= LS - ES = LF - EF\). It belongs to the path, not the activity.
  • Free slack \(= \min(ES_{\text{successors}}) - EF\). Usable without moving any successor.
  • Critical path = longest path = project duration. Recalculate after every change.
  • Lags: FS + L, SS + L, FF + L are minimum separations. A lag is not hidden work.
  • Time runs from zero. EF = 2 means the successor may start at 2.

Two critical paths

Path Length
A – B – D – G – H 18
A – C – E – G – H 18
A – C – F – H 11

D and E both finish on day 11. Both branches are critical.

  • Shorten D by two days: the project is still 18. E controls.
  • Shorten D and E by two days each: 16.
  • Compression must shorten every controlling path, and the result must be recalculated each time.

On Thursday the network has one critical path. The tie here is the lesson, not the format.

Answer · how likely is day 22?

Both critical paths carry the same variance: \(1 + 1 + 9 + 1 + 4 = 16\), so \(\sigma_{T_E} = 4\).

Question Working Result
Finish by day 22 \(Z = (22 - 18)/4 = 1.00\) 84%
Finish by day 18 \(Z = 0\) 50%
90 percent confidence \(T_S = 18 + 1.28 \times 4 = 23.1\) 24 days

Variances were added along one path, not across the whole network (that sum would be 27).

Because two paths tie, the project must wait for both at G. The single-path formula ignores that and is optimistic; a simulation would put the chance of finishing by day 22 below 84 percent.

Practice D · multiple choice

B has 5 days of total slack and 0 days of free slack. Its only successor, D, has 5 days of total slack and 5 of free slack. Which statement is correct?

  1. B can be delayed five days without affecting any other activity
  2. B and D each have an independent five-day reserve, ten days in total
  3. Delaying B by three days delays D’s early start by three days and leaves two days of slack on the branch
  4. D is critical because its free slack equals its total slack

Answer

(c). Total slack belongs to the path. B’s zero free slack says D starts the moment B finishes, so any delay to B moves D. The branch has one five-day allowance, shared; giving each activity its own counts it twice.

The exam asks for free slack on two activities and then poses a “what if D slips” question. Work it as a new forward pass from the changed activity.

Chapter 5 · Estimating project times and costs

Week 5 notes, LO 5-1 to LO 5-8 and Appendix 5.1

What the estimate handed to the schedule

  • A duration for each work package, under stated normal conditions, estimated independently of its neighbours.
  • Effort and duration kept apart: 40 labour-hours is not 5 days, and two people do not make it 2.5.
  • Contingency held visibly, so the schedule’s reserve is one number the project manager can see and govern.

Carry these

  • Range · accuracy · precision · confidence are four different claims. 17.4 days is precise. “10–15 days” and the task took 9: inaccurate.
  • Seven guidelines: responsibility near the work; several estimators; normal conditions; consistent units; packages estimated independently; contingency visible; risk assessed before commitment.
  • Top-down fits strategy, high uncertainty, unstable scope, small internal work. Bottom-up fits fixed price, critical commitments, detail the customer requires.

Carry these, continued

  • Direct cost · direct project overhead · G&A overhead. Committed cost shows the future before actual cost does.
  • Reference class forecasting shifts the burden of proof: the advocates must say why this project will beat the base rate.
  • White elephant: would we spend the remaining money for the remaining benefit? Sunk cost explains the past; it does not buy the next dollar.

Practice F · multiple choice

Which statement is about precision rather than accuracy, range or confidence?

  1. “The job will take 10 to 15 days.”
  2. “The job will take 12.5 days.”
  3. “The job took 11 days, inside the 10-to-15 estimate.”
  4. “There is a 90 percent chance the job finishes by Friday.”

Answer

(b). A point value to one decimal is a precise statement and says nothing about whether it is right. (a) is a range, (c) is an accuracy judgement made after the fact, (d) is a confidence claim.

The exam’s version of this question turns on accuracy: an estimate is judged against the range it stated, and finishing early outside the range is still inaccurate.

Practice G · short answer

Early in a project the actual-cost line sits comfortably under the scheduled budget. Why can committed cost be the more informative number at that point?

Full credit: committed cost counts what the project has obligated through contracts and purchase orders before any invoice arrives; it therefore rises before actual cost and shows future spending now; a project can look under budget on actuals while signed contracts have already consumed the remaining authorization. Mention that all three lines meet at the same total.

Chapter 4 · Defining the project

Week 4 notes, LO 4-1 to LO 4-8

What definition handed to estimating

  • Work packages that answer five questions: what, how long, what cost, what resources, who owns it. Decomposition stops there.
  • WBS codes so that estimates, budgets and actuals aggregate at any level.
  • Cost accounts: a work package joined to the organization unit accountable for it.
  • A priority matrix so the estimator knows what the project will protect when the numbers collide.

Carry these

  • The scope checklist: objective, product description, justification, deliverables, milestones, technical requirements, limits and exclusions, acceptance criteria.
  • Deliverable is an output; milestone is a control point with no duration; technical requirement constrains performance; acceptance criterion is the evidence for yes; exclusion stops assumed work entering by silence.
  • Scope creep is a change whose consequences were not deliberately accepted. Gold plating is the team’s own creep.
  • Constrain · enhance · accept. Marking everything “constrain” is a refusal to set priorities.

Carry these, continued

  • WBS tests: coverage (children add to the parent) and manageability (stop when it can be estimated and owned).
  • PBS when phases organize the work. Responsibility matrix needs exactly one R per row.
  • Communication plan ends with “who provides it.” Power × interest sets the intensity.

Practice J · multiple choice

The sponsor marks time, cost and performance all as constrain on the priority matrix. The best description of what has happened is:

  1. The project now has three protected objectives and no risk
  2. The sponsor has refused to set priorities, so the project manager will improvise the ranking the first time pressure appears
  3. Quality has been moved to the centre of the triangle
  4. The project is a compliance project

Answer

(b). The labels are comparative. If nothing may move, the first trade-off is decided by whoever is in the room at the time, which is the situation the matrix exists to prevent.

Chapter 3 · Organization: structure and culture

Week 3 notes, LO 3-1 to LO 3-7

What structure handed to definition

  • Someone who can own a work package: the OBS is only real if the unit on it has the authority and the budget.
  • The project manager’s authority over the people who will execute the plan. Weak matrix: influence. Strong matrix or dedicated team: direction.
  • A culture that either supplies cooperation across units for free, or does not.

Carry these

Structure One advantage One disadvantage
Functional no change, deep expertise, easy transition no focus, no owner, slow, poor integration
Dedicated team fast, cohesive, integrated expensive, projectitis, limited depth, hard transition
Matrix efficient sharing, strong focus, flexible two bosses, infighting, stress, slow decisions

Each column is the same fact seen from two directions: what makes a structure cheap is what leaves the project without an owner.

Carry these, continued

  • Weak · balanced · strong matrix: coordinator with influence; what vs how; project manager controls assignments and trade-offs.
  • PMO facilitates and enables; it does not do the project’s scheduling, planning and budgeting.
  • Seven factors raise the autonomy needed. Factor 7, stable resource requirements, is the one that argues the other way.
  • Ten cultural dimensions; three want the middle: control, management focus, means-ends.
  • Structure and culture are substitutes. Read the culture before choosing the structure.

Practice K · multiple choice

Under which condition is a strong matrix preferable to a dedicated project team?

  1. The project is large, novel, and strategically critical, and the organization’s culture is hostile to cross-unit work
  2. Scarce specialists must be shared across several projects, and the organization wants the “feel” of a project team without carving the specialists out of their functions
  3. The project will run for ten years with the same staff
  4. The organization has no PMO

Answer

(b). The strong matrix is the chapter’s attempt to create the feel of a project team inside the functional hierarchy: the project manager directs the work, the specialists keep their functional home, and the organization keeps sharing them. Condition (a) is the case for a dedicated team, because that culture will not supply what the matrix needs.

Practice L · short answer

Why should a project manager read the organization’s culture before choosing a project structure?

Full credit: culture determines whether cross-unit requests are normal or presumptuous; a supportive culture supplies focus, integration and trade-off authority at no cost, so a light structure works; an unsupportive one withholds them, so the project must be separated out and given its own authority; a mismatch is information about how much structural protection the project needs, not an obstacle.

Chapter 2 · Organization strategy and project selection

Week 2 notes, LO 2-1 to LO 2-9

What selection handed to structure

  • A classified project: compliance, operational, or strategic, with its share of the portfolio decided before ranking.
  • A gate 4 implementation plan that was judged twice, the second time with real numbers.
  • A published priority, so that the structure it lands in knows how hard to fight for its people.

Carry these

  • Four activities of strategic management: mission → strategies → objectives → projects. Only the fourth is visible to a customer.
  • SMART; assignable is the one that gets dropped.
  • Six symptoms of a missing priority system; “resources are not adequate” is the trap.
  • Optimism bias flatters the estimate; uniqueness bias explains away the base rate.
  • Phase gates: go · kill · recycle. Gate 5 is the one organizations skip.
  • Payback is blind to time value and to everything after the cutoff. NPV counts every flow at present value.
  • Must objectives screen; want objectives are weighted. The weights relocate judgement; they do not remove it.
  • Portfolio matrix: bread and butter · pearl · oyster · white elephant. Balance, not ranking.

Practice M · short answer

Two projects cost the same. Payback prefers Y; net present value prefers X. Both calculations are correct. Explain how that can be, and say when a firm would be right to choose Y anyway.

Full credit: payback measures how long the money is exposed and ignores the time value of money and everything after the cutoff; NPV discounts every flow and counts the later cash that Y stops producing; a firm short of cash may rightly prefer Y, but it cannot choose Y believing Y is the more valuable project.

Practice N · multiple choice

A priority team ranks four proposals with a weighted scoring model. The totals fall within fifteen points of each other, and one member shows that moving a single weight by ten points reverses the order. What should the team conclude?

  1. The model has failed and the proposals should be ranked on NPV alone
  2. The original weights stand, because they were published before scoring
  3. The judgement has been relocated into the weights, where it is visible and arguable; this ranking has not separated its candidates, so the argument should move to the criteria
  4. The proposals should be reclassified as must objectives

Answer

(c). A total weighted score is not an objective fact. The gain of the model is that the argument now happens over the criteria, in writing, rather than over the projects. A ranking that flips under a plausible change in weights is telling the team that the proposals are not yet distinguishable on the stated criteria.

Practice O · multiple choice

A product that was a pearl three years ago now aims at a market that has since saturated, and it keeps its budget because of the reputation it earned earlier. In the portfolio matrix it is now a

  1. bread-and-butter project
  2. oyster
  3. white elephant
  4. compliance project

Answer

(c). Nobody funds a white elephant on purpose. It is a pearl or an oyster that stopped being viable while nobody was looking. Nothing in its own status report says this quadrant has changed, which is the argument for reviewing the portfolio rather than the project.

Chapter 1 · Modern project management

Week 1 notes, LO 1-1 to LO 1-5

What chapter 1 handed to everything

  • A project: a temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service or result.
  • Five characteristics: an objective; a defined lifespan; several departments and professionals; something not done before; specific time, cost and performance requirements.
  • The triple constraint, which every later chapter trades across.
  • The life cycle: defining · planning · executing · closing. Effort peaks in executing; the chapters you were examined on live in defining and planning.
  • Plan-driven vs agile: predictability of scope and technology decides. Hybrid blends them. Agile suits small teams, four to eight.
  • Technical and sociocultural dimensions. Strength in one does not cover weakness in the other.

Carry these

  • Project vs operations: writing the piano piece, not practising scales.
  • Program: related projects under a common goal; the difference is scale and time span.
  • Closing has three jobs: deliver, redeploy, review (lessons learned).
  • Drivers of demand: compressed product life cycles, the knowledge explosion, the triple bottom line, the multiproject environment. Small projects carry hidden costs the accounting system never sees.
  • The WBS is a database, not a diagram. That claim was made in Week 1 and cashed in Week 4.

Practice P · multiple choice

A refinery runs a maintenance shutdown every spring: same crews, same checklist, a fixed window, a fixed budget. Which of the five characteristics does it most clearly fail, and is it still a project?

  1. A defined lifespan; no, it is operations
  2. Something never done before; yes, uniqueness is a matter of degree and the time, cost and performance requirements are specific
  3. Specific requirements; no, because the budget is fixed
  4. Several departments; yes, because only one crew is involved

Answer

(b). The fourth characteristic is “not an either/or issue but a matter of degree.” Each shutdown is customised to what the inspection finds, and it carries exactly the accountability the triple constraint describes. The weakest characteristic does not disqualify the project; the pattern of the five does.

Missing link What the risk register looks like
