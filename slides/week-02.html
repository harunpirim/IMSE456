LO 2-1 · Why the strategy is your business

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 2

Last week, and this week

Week 1: what a project is, and how to manage it well.

This week: why this project and not another one.

A project executed flawlessly against the wrong objective is still waste.

Strategy only becomes visible as projects

A strategy that stays in the plan changes nothing.

It becomes real as new products, new services, new processes — and those arrive as projects.

Why the climate tightened the fit

  • Rapid technology change
  • Global competition
  • Financial uncertainty
  • Disease outbreaks

Each shortens the time you have to notice your projects no longer serve your direction.

Two reasons you cannot outsource this

Make the right call when the plan runs out. Every project throws up decisions the plan did not anticipate.

Argue for the project. Resources are contested. That case is made in the language of the organization’s objectives, or not at all.

LO 2-3 · Why a priority system

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 2

Six symptoms of a disconnect

  • Frequent conflict between functional managers; trust erodes
  • Meetings called repeatedly to renegotiate priorities
  • People moved between projects as priorities shift
  • Nobody can say which projects matter
  • Several projects each, and ineffective on all of them
  • Resources are not adequate

The trap in the last one

Resources are not adequate sounds like a budget problem.

If the cause is that too many projects were approved, more resources fund the same disorder at a larger scale.

Two biases that keep the pipeline full

Optimism bias — expecting better outcomes than the record justifies.

Uniqueness bias — seeing yourself as more singular than you are.

Kahneman (2011), Thinking, Fast and Slow

Alicke & Govorun (2005); Suls & Wan (1987)

They compound

Optimism bias makes the estimate flattering.

Uniqueness bias explains away the evidence that it is flattering.

A priority system is partly a defence against your own judgement.

What portfolio management buys

  • Discipline in selection
  • Selection linked to strategic metrics
  • Common criteria, not politics or emotion
  • Resources aligned to direction
  • Risk balanced across projects
  • Justification for killing projects
  • Better communication on goals

Each is justified differently

Kind Justified by
Compliance / emergency The penalty for not doing it
Operational The saving or improvement
Strategic Contribution to the strategy

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 2

They cannot share a scale

Rank all three on net present value and compliance work always loses.

That is not a finding about compliance work. It is a broken ranking.

The fix: decide the split between categories before ranking anything inside them.

LO 2-5 · The phase gate model

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 2

A gate has three outcomes

Redrawn from Larson & Gray, Ch. 2, phase gate exhibit

Six phases

Phase The gate asks
1 · Idea Worth a proposal?
2 · Proposal Does it clear the screen?
3 · Screening and selection Funded, at what priority?
4 · Implementation plan Does the plan hold up?
5 · Progress evaluation Continue, or stop?
6 · Closure Genuinely finished?

Gate 4 · where hope meets numbers

Once the preliminary proposal is approved, a manager and staff are assigned to build out the plan in full.

Does the plan make sense? Do the numbers add up? Is it worth the risk? How much confidence is there in the plan?

The gate 4 questions, Larson & Gray, Ch. 2

Gate 5 · the one that gets skipped

It exists so a project can be cancelled for poor performance or lost relevance.

Applied to work already underway, and already defended.

Scrutiny scales: a three-year DoD project reviews every six months; small projects need nothing heavier than a superior’s approval.

LO 2-6 · What a project is worth

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 2

Payback

\[\text{Payback} = \frac{\text{Estimated cost}}{\text{Annual net cash inflow}}\]

Quick · no discount rate needed · everyone understands it

Blind twice: ignores the time value of money, and everything after the cutoff.

Net present value

\[\text{NPV} = -I_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{F_t}{(1+k)^t}\]

Positive NPV: earns more than the required return.

Negative NPV: it does not.

Two projects, $180,000 each, 12%

Year A B
1 $60,000 $90,000
2 $70,000 $90,000
3 $70,000 $10,000
4 $50,000 $10,000

They disagree

A B
Payback 2.71 years 2.00 years
NPV at 12% +$10,976 −$14,422

Both are correct. They answer different questions.

What payback cannot see

B returns cash quickly and then stops.

A cash-poor firm may still choose B and be right to.

What it cannot do is choose B believing B is the more valuable project.

Non-financial criteria

Firms fund thin-margin projects to:

  • Capture market share
  • Raise the barrier to a competitor
  • Build an enabler product that lifts other sales
  • Develop core technology for the next generation
  • Reduce dependence on an unreliable supplier
  • Head off regulation

And the discipline runs both ways

Companies must be able to say no to a profitable project outside the mission.

Hard, because the case for it is concrete — real revenue, a real customer.

The case against it is the abstraction the mission exists to protect.

LO 2-7 · Multi-criteria models

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 2

Two kinds of criterion

Must objectives — pass or fail. Never traded. A screen, not a contribution.

Want objectives — weighted 1–100, impact scored 0/1/2 against published definitions.

Published definitions are the point

0 = does not address · 1 = opportunity to fix · 2 = urgent problem solved

Two people apply that to the same proposal and get the same score.

“Rate the impact 0 to 2” does not.

A model

Want objective Weight Alpha Beta Gamma
Reduces unplanned downtime 90 2 1 0
Opens a new customer segment 70 0 2 2
Improves regulatory margin 55 1 0 2
Total 235 230 250

Gamma first, Alpha second, Beta third.

Now move one weight

Downtime: 90 → 100

Alpha Beta Gamma
Total 255 240 250

The ranking inverts. Same proposals, same impact scores.

What that means

A weighted model does not remove judgement.

It relocates judgement into the weights — written down, visible, arguable.

That is a real gain. But a total weighted score is not an objective fact.

Two evaluations, not one

Estimates get worse as they get detailed

$10 million becomes $12 million. A key feature drops out.

Significant negative differences go up to senior management to decide whether it is still approved.

The system expects this and forces a second decision.

Keeping the system honest

  • Every significant project ranked, and the ranking published
  • Senior management actively supports it
  • Going around it destroys it
  • Review boards ask hard questions
  • Resources allocated explicitly, not as overload

LO 2-9 · The portfolio as a portfolio

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 2

A project is judged in context

Merits are assessed within the context of the projects that already exist.

Competing for the same skilled people · dispersed teams · time-to-market pressure · limited capital

None of it is visible from inside a single project.

Senior management supplies two things

Criteria aligned to current strategy.

An annual balance decision — say 20% compliance, 50% strategic, 30% operational — made before selection begins.

Governance and open information

A governance team publishes every project’s priority, status and open issues.

Open communication discourages power plays.

Influence works best unobserved.

The portfolio matrix

Redrawn from Larson & Gray, Ch. 2, portfolio matrix exhibit; matrix originates with Matheson & Matheson (1998)

Where white elephants come from

No organization funds one deliberately.

A white elephant is a pearl or an oyster that stopped being viable while nobody was looking.

It keeps its budget through the reputation it earned earlier.

Which is the argument for reviewing the portfolio

Nothing inside a white elephant’s own status report says this quadrant has changed.

The uncomfortable conclusion

A highly ranked project can be deferred or refused if it upsets the balance of risk, resources and strategy.

The best-scoring proposal in the room is not automatically the one to fund.

And the person whose proposal is deferred did nothing wrong.
