1 · What counts as a project

Temporary and unique

A temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result.

  • Temporary — a definite beginning and end. Bounded, not brief.
  • Unique — not previously produced in this configuration. Not unprecedented.

Projects are the kind of work you can be publicly wrong about in advance.

Operations are the other thing

Operations Projects
Shape Ongoing, repetitive Bounded, unrepeated
Task Optimize a known process Execute an unrepeated one
Basis You have data You have inference

The boundary cases

  • A film production — temporary and unique, but the studio makes one every year.
  • A software release train — each release unique, the cadence operational.
  • A refinery turnaround — recurring every four years, planned as a project every time.

“Project or operation?” is not a fact about the world. It is a management choice about which toolkit to apply — and you can be wrong about it.

Program and portfolio

  • Program — related projects managed together because of dependencies between them.
  • Portfolio — projects and programs grouped because they compete for the same money.

If there are no dependencies, it is a portfolio and someone chose a more impressive word.

2 · The iron triangle, and how it lies

Failure 1 — a constraint model, not a success model

Meeting all three legs says nothing about whether the output was worth building.

Sydney Opera House

1957 estimate £3.5 million (≈ A$7 million)
Scheduled completion 26 January 1963
Actually opened 20 October 1973
Final cost A$102 million

Roughly fourteen times the estimate, ten years late — and not remembered as a failure.

Failure 2 — it hides the asymmetry

The three legs are drawn as equals. They are not.

  • The date is contractual, regulatory, or attached to a season.
  • The budget was approved in public and must be re-approved in public.
  • Scope lives in a document, and documents can be edited quietly.

The failure is not that scope flexes. It is that it flexes without anyone recording that it flexed.

Failure 3 — there is no stakeholder in it

Time. Cost. Scope. Whose?

The sponsor, the user, the team and the neighbour can read the same finished project and disagree. The triangle has no room to express that, so it silently adopts one view and calls it the answer.

Callback to the pavilion: same three numbers, opposite verdicts, and the disagreement was the case.

So why does it survive?

Because it is the only success model you can put in a contract.

Scope, time and cost can be agreed in advance and adjudicated afterwards. “Did we produce value” cannot. The triangle is legible, not complete — keep it as the constraint set, not the scorecard.

3 · Success, in more than one dimension

Five dimensions, five clocks

Dimension The question When you can answer
Project efficiency Did we hit time and budget? Immediately
Impact on the customer Does it meet requirements — and get used? Months
Impact on the team Did the team grow, or burn out? Months
Business / direct success Commercial or mission value? 1–2 years
Preparing for the future Capability, technology, position? 3–5 years

Shenhar & Dvir, Reinventing Project Management (2007). The 2001 Long Range Planning paper has four; “impact on the team” was added in the book.

Row one is the whole iron triangle

The dimension you can measure soonest is the dimension you get judged on.

The dimensions that would vindicate or condemn you arrive after everyone has moved on.

The number everyone quotes

Flyvbjerg’s database, 16,000+ projects:

  • 0.5% — on budget, on time, and with the promised benefits
  • 8.5% — on budget and on time
  • 47.9% — on budget alone

Mean cost overrun: nuclear waste storage +238% · Olympics +157% · nuclear power +120% · IT +73% · buildings +62% · rail +39% · tunnels +37% · wind +13% · solar +1%

Flyvbjerg & Gardner, How Big Things Get Done (2023); method in Flyvbjerg (2014), PMJ 45(2), 6–19.

Now immediately complicate it

  • Chapman (2024), PMJ 55(6) — Swedish transport infrastructure, 228 road + 96 rail. Typical road overrun 2.7%; rail a 1.8% underrun. 93% of road and 70% of rail achieved intended benefits.
  • Chapman (2025), IJPM 43(8) — 125 rail + 280 road projects across Denmark, Norway and Sweden, 2008–2022: delivered on cost, over time.

Base rates are not properties of projects. They are properties of a governance regime.

Four questions for any failure statistic

  1. Which projects — sector, size, era?
  2. Failed against what — the triangle, or benefits?
  3. Who assembled the sample, and would the successes report themselves?
  4. Compared to what — what is the unmanaged counterfactual?

Change the instrument, change the finding

Gemino, Horner Reich & Serrador (2021), PMJ 52(2), n = 477:

Agile and hybrid approaches significantly improved stakeholder success over traditional — while producing statistically the same budget, time, scope and quality outcomes.

Measure only the triangle and you conclude approach doesn’t matter. Nothing about the projects changed between those two conclusions. Only the instrument did.

4 · Standards, tailoring, and where the tools sit

PMBOK 8 — and the interesting part

Released by PMI late 2025; general availability January 2026.

  • 6 principles (down from 12) — including integrate sustainability, now standalone for the first time
  • 7 performance domains (down from 8) — Governance, Scope, Schedule, Finance, Stakeholders, Resources, Risk
  • 40 processes, reintroduced in deliberately non-prescriptive form

49 → 0 → 40

Edition Year Shape
PMBOK 6 2017 49 processes, 5 process groups, 10 knowledge areas
PMBOK 7 2021 Processes removed; principle-based
PMBOK 8 2025 Processes back, non-prescriptive; tailoring mandatory

Processes give you something to do and nothing to think with. Principles give you the reverse. Version 8 is an attempt to hold both.

Four different kinds of document

Document Claims to be Governance stance
PMBOK 8 (PMI, 2025) Body of knowledge + standard; descriptive Tailoring mandatory
PRINCE2 (PeopleCert) A method — prescriptive processes and roles Strong: board, stage boundaries, exceptions
ISO 21502:2020 Guidance — generic, vendor-neutral, non-certifiable Light; written for contracts
Scrum Guide (Nov 2020) A framework — immutable, deliberately incomplete None beyond the team

Which of these could you sue somebody over? That tells you where the accountability sits.

The counterexample

The Scrum Guide: 13 pages, free, last revised November 2020, still current.

Added the Product Goal and gave each artifact one commitment — Product Backlog → Product Goal, Sprint Backlog → Sprint Goal, Increment → Definition of Done.

Students assume standards churn. The ones with exam revenue attached do. Separate “revised because knowledge advanced” from “revised because there is a certification.”

Tailoring — five questions, every time

  1. How much will requirements change before delivery? High → adaptive
  2. How expensive is a late change? Very → predictive front-loading
  3. Can we deliver increments a stakeholder would accept? No → predictive
  4. Is there a gate requiring a frozen baseline? Yes → predictive at the gate
  5. Is the team co-located and empowered enough to self-manage? No → the ceremonies are theatre
