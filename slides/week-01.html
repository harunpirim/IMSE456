LO 1-1 · Why this is crucial

A good time to be reading this

Project management is critical to sustainable economic growth.

New jobs and competitive advantage are achieved by constant innovation — new products and services, better productivity and quality of work.

More than a set of tools

Project management gives people a powerful set of tools to plan, implement and manage activities to accomplish specific objectives.

But it is more than that:

A contact sport that requires building collaborative relationships among a diverse cast of stakeholders.

Where it came from

Long the style of doing business in:

  • The construction industry
  • The U.S. Department of Defense
  • Hollywood
  • Big consulting firms

Where it went

Port expansions · hospital restructuring · upgrading information systems.

Next-generation fuel-efficient vehicles · sustainable energy · the farthest reaches of outer space.

Impact is most profound in high-tech industries.

Not just the private sector

A vehicle for doing good deeds and solving social problems:

  • Emergency aid to areas hit by natural disasters
  • A strategy for reducing crime and drug abuse within a city
  • A community effort to renovate a public playground

The numbers

PMI membership, 2002 93,000
PMI membership, 2021 686,000+
U.S. annual spend on projects ~$2.5 trillion
Share of U.S. gross national product ~25%

Most never hold the title

Accountants · lawyers · administrators · scientists · contractors · coaches · public health officials · teachers · community advocates.

For some the work is project driven — cases for lawyers, audits for accountants, events for artists, renovations for contractors.

For others, a small but critical part

  • A teacher with four classes a day, coaching a national debate team
  • A store manager developing an employee retention programme
  • A sales exec launching daily deals into a new city
  • A public health official organising a Homeless Youth Connect event

Not a title. A job requirement.

The skill transfers

It is hard to think of a profession or a career path that would not benefit from being good at managing projects.

Project management fundamentals are universal.

Each person is likely to experience three to four career changes.

And it shows up in the classroom

Twenty years ago: one or two classes, primarily for engineers.

Today: multiple sections — engineers supplemented by marketing, MIS and finance, plus oceanography, health sciences, computer sciences and liberal arts.

Employers are looking for graduates with project management skills.

LO 1-2 · What a project is

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 1

What do these have in common?

  • Millions Watch World Cup Finals
  • Citywide WiFi System Set to Go Live
  • Hospitals Respond to New Healthcare Reforms
  • Apple’s New iPhone Hits the Market
  • City Receives Stimulus Funds to Expand Light Rail System

All of these events are projects.

The definition

A project is a temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result.

Like most organisational efforts, the major goal is to satisfy a customer’s need.

Five characteristics

#
1 An established objective
2 A defined lifespan with a beginning and an end
3 Usually several departments and professionals
4 Typically something never done before
5 Specific time, cost and performance requirements

1 · An established objective

A 12-storey apartment complex by January 1.

Version 2.0 of a software package as quickly as possible.

This singular purpose is often lacking in daily organisational life, where workers perform repetitive operations each day.

2 · A defined endpoint

Contrary to the ongoing duties of traditional jobs.

Instead of staying in one job, individuals move from project to project, working with different groups of people.

After installing a security system, an IT engineer may be assigned to develop a database for a different client.

3 · Combined effort of specialists

Unlike work segmented by functional speciality.

Engineers · financial analysts · marketing professionals · quality control specialists.

Instead of separate offices under separate managers, they work together under the guidance of a project manager.

4 · Nonroutine, with unique elements

Not either/or — a matter of degree.

An electric automobile, or two rovers on Mars: previously unsolved problems and breakthrough technology.

Even basic construction projects with established routines require some degree of customisation that makes them unique.

5 · Time, cost and performance

Projects are evaluated according to accomplishment, cost and time spent.

These triple constraints impose a higher degree of accountability than is typically found in most jobs.

And they name the primary function: balancing the trade-offs among time, cost and performance while ultimately satisfying the customer.

What a project is not

Routine, repetitive Project
Taking class notes Writing a term paper
Entering sales receipts Setting up a sales kiosk for a meeting
Responding to a supply-chain request Developing a supply-chain information system
Practising scales on the piano Writing a new piano piece
Routine manufacture of a music player Designing one that stores 10,000 songs

Why the distinction earns its keep

Too often resources can be used up on daily operations, which may not contribute to the longer-range organisation strategies that require innovative new products.

Program versus project

A program is a group of related projects designed to accomplish a common goal over an extended period of time.

Each project within a program has a project manager.

The major differences lie in scale and time span. Program goals are closely related to broad strategic organisation goals.

LO 1-3 · The project life cycle

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 1

Predictable changes in effort and focus

Many life-cycle models are unique to an industry or project type.

New software development: definition · design · code · integration/test · maintenance.

A generic cycle has four stages.

1 · Defining

Specifications of the project are defined.

Project objectives are established.

Teams are formed.

Major responsibilities are assigned.

2 · Planning

The level of effort increases.

Plans are developed to determine what the project will entail, when it will be scheduled, whom it will benefit, what quality level should be maintained, and what the budget will be.

3 · Executing

A major portion of the project work — physical and mental.

The physical product is produced: a bridge, a report, a software program.

Time, cost and specification measures are used for control. On schedule? On budget? Meeting specifications? What are the forecasts? What revisions are necessary?

4 · Closing

Three activities:

  1. Delivering the project product to the customer — customer training, transferring documents
  2. Redeploying project resources — releasing equipment and materials, finding new assignments for team members
  3. Post-project review — assessing performance and capturing lessons learned

What each stage produces

Defining Planning Executing Closing
Goals Schedules Status reports Train customer
Specifications Budgets Changes Transfer documents
Tasks Resources Quality Release resources
Responsibilities Risks Forecasts Evaluation
Staffing Lessons learned

The project manager

Project managers are often stars in their organisation and well compensated.

They develop, or are given, a project charter or scope statement defining the objectives and parameters of the project.

They work with others to create a schedule and budget that guide execution — and must understand project priorities so they can make independent decisions.

LO 1-4 · Agile project management

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 1

Traditional PM assumes predictability

Traditional project management focuses on thorough planning up front.

Planning requires predictability. Managers must understand what is to be accomplished and how to do it.

Building a bridge, engineers draw upon proven technology and design principles.

Not all projects enjoy such predictability.

Project uncertainty

Uncertainty varies according to two things:

  • The extent the project scope (What) is known and stable
  • The extent the technology (How) is known and proven

Predictable → unpredictable → unknown.

Where planning works

Bridges · product extensions · events · marketing campaigns.

Well-established scopes, proven technology — which provides predictability for effective planning.

When scope and/or technology is not fully known, plan-driven methods suffer.

The cost of ignoring it

$81 billion — spent by American firms and agencies on cancelled software projects, 1995 (Standish Group)

> $2 trillion — cost of poor software quality in the U.S., 2021, from unsuccessful projects, software failure, and inferior quality legacy systems (Krasner, 2022)

Rolling wave

An incremental, iterative process.

Instead of planning everything up front, the scope evolves. The final design and outcome is not known in great detail, and is continuously developed through incremental iterations — waves.

Iterations typically last one to four weeks.

Each iteration

Makes tangible progress — defining a key requirement, solving a technical problem, creating features to demonstrate to the customer.

At the end, progress is reviewed, adjustments are made, a new cycle begins.

Each new iteration subsumes the work of the previous ones until the project is complete and the customer satisfied.

Agile in one line

Active collaboration between the project team and customer representatives · breaking projects into small functional pieces · adapting to changing requirements.

Agile works best in small teams of four to eight members.

Not simply either/or

Agile up front in the defining phase to establish specifications and requirements.

Traditional methods to plan, execute and close.

Agile also used to address certain technical issues while most of the work is managed traditionally.

Hybrid project management is the name for this blending.

Forces driving demand

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 1

Compression of the product life cycle

High-tech product life cycles now average six months to three years. Only 30 years ago, 10 to 15 years was not uncommon.

Rule of thumb: a six-month project delay can cost 33 percent of product revenue share.

Speed becomes a competitive advantage.

Knowledge explosion

Building a road 30 years ago was somewhat simple.

Today: materials, specifications, codes, aesthetics, equipment, required specialists — every area more complex.

Hard to find a new product without at least one microchip. The same is likely true for AI.

Project management has emerged as the key discipline for integrating divergent technologies.

Triple bottom line

Planet · people · profit.

Businesses can no longer simply focus on maximising profit to the detriment of the environment and society.

Reducing carbon imprints and using renewable resources are realised through effective project management — a high LEED certification is often an objective in its own right.

Small projects, big problems

Hundreds of projects implemented concurrently — a multiproject environment.

Sharing and prioritising resources across a portfolio is a major challenge for senior management.

Why small projects hide

  • They carry the same or more risk as large projects
  • They are perceived as low-impact — they don’t demand scarce resources or money
  • Because so many run concurrently and each impact seems small, measuring inefficiency is usually nonexistent
  • They soon add up to large sums; customers and millions of dollars are lost each year

Hidden costs not measured in the accounting system.

The technical dimension

Project management methodology: planning, scheduling, controlling.

Scope statements link the project and customer, and facilitate planning and control.

Think of the WBS as a database, not a diagram — it links every level of the organisation to every deliverable and every piece of work, down to individual tasks.

Because the links are built in, a change anywhere can be traced back to whatever caused it. That traceability is the payoff.

The sociocultural dimension

Where planning is orderly, implementation is messy, frequently contradictory, often paradoxical.

Build a culture that keeps people working together and invested — and that surfaces problems early enough to act on.

Things rarely go as planned. Steer back on track, or alter direction when necessary.

The social network

Build the social network deliberately — across stakeholders who hold different standards, are committed to different things, and see the same project from incompatible angles.

Neither half covers for the other.

Produce a plan and a budget — and orchestrate people you mostly do not control.
