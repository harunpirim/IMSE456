LO 1-1 · Why project management is crucial

Larson and Gray have opened every edition of the text with the same line: this is a good time to be reading a book about project management. It is more true now than when it was written [1].

Business leaders and experts recognise that project management is critical to sustainable economic growth. New jobs and competitive advantage are achieved by constant innovation — developing new products and services, and improving both the productivity and the quality of work. That is the world of project management.

Project management gives people a powerful set of tools that improves their ability to plan, implement and manage activities to accomplish specific objectives. But it is more than a set of tools:

Project management is a contact sport that requires building collaborative relationships among a diverse cast of stakeholders.

Exciting opportunities await people skilled in it.

Where the project approach came from, and where it went

The project approach has long been the style of doing business in the construction industry, the U.S. Department of Defense, Hollywood, and big consulting firms. It has now spread to all avenues of work.

Today project teams carry out everything from port expansions to hospital restructuring to upgrading information systems. They are creating next-generation fuel-efficient vehicles, developing sustainable sources of energy, and exploring the farthest reaches of outer space.

The impact is most profound in high-tech industries, where the new folk heroes are young professionals whose Herculean efforts lead to the constant flow of new hardware and software products.

Project management is not limited to the private sector. It is a vehicle for doing good deeds and solving social problems — providing emergency aid to areas hit by natural disasters, devising a strategy for reducing crime and drug abuse within a city, or organising a community effort to renovate a public playground. All would and do benefit from the application of modern project management techniques.

The numbers

Indicator Figure
PMI membership, 2002 93,000
PMI membership, 2021 more than 686,000
Annual U.S. spend on projects approximately $2.5 trillion
That spend as a share of U.S. gross national product about 25 percent

Other countries are increasingly spending more on projects. Millions of people around the world consider project management the major task in their profession.

Most people who manage projects never hold the title

Many people who excel at managing projects never have the title project manager. They include accountants, lawyers, administrators, scientists, contractors, coaches, public health officials, teachers and community advocates whose success depends upon being able to lead and manage project work.

For some, the very nature of the work is project driven — cases for lawyers, audits for accountants, events for artists, renovations for contractors.

For others, projects are a small but critical part of the work:

  • A high school teacher who teaches four classes a day is responsible for coaching a group of students to compete in a national debate competition.
  • A store manager who oversees daily operations is charged with developing an employee retention programme.
  • A sales account executive is given the additional assignment of team lead to launch daily deals into a new city.
  • A public health official who manages a clinic is also responsible for organising a Homeless Youth Connect event.

For these and others, project management is not a title but a critical job requirement.

It is hard to think of a profession or a career path that would not benefit from being good at managing projects.

The skill transfers

Not only is project management critical to most careers, the skill set is transferable across most businesses and professions. Project management fundamentals are universal. The same methodology used to develop a new product can be adapted to create new services, organise events, refurbish ageing operations, and so forth.

In a world where it is estimated that each person is likely to experience three to four career changes, managing projects is a talent worthy of development.

And it shows up in the classroom

Twenty years ago, major universities offered one or two classes in project management, primarily for engineers. Today most universities offer multiple sections, with the core group of engineers supplemented by business students majoring in marketing, management information systems (MIS) and finance, as well as students from oceanography, health sciences, computer sciences and liberal arts.

These students are finding that their exposure to project management provides distinct advantages when it comes time to look for jobs. More and more employers are looking for graduates with project management skills.

LO 1-2 · What a project is

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 1 [1].

Start here

What do the following headlines have in common?

  • Millions Watch World Cup Finals
  • Citywide WiFi System Set to Go Live
  • Hospitals Respond to New Healthcare Reforms
  • Apple’s New iPhone Hits the Market
  • City Receives Stimulus Funds to Expand Light Rail System

All of these events are projects.

The definition

The Project Management Institute provides the following definition [4]:

A project is a temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result.

Like most organisational efforts, the major goal of a project is to satisfy a customer’s need. Beyond that fundamental similarity, the characteristics of a project help differentiate it from other endeavours of the organisation.

The five characteristics

# Characteristic
1 An established objective
2 A defined lifespan with a beginning and an end
3 Usually, the involvement of several departments and professionals
4 Typically, doing something that has never been done before
5 Specific time, cost, and performance requirements

First — a defined objective. Whether it is constructing a 12-storey apartment complex by January 1 or releasing version 2.0 of a specific software package as quickly as possible. This singular purpose is often lacking in daily organisational life, where workers perform repetitive operations each day.

Second — a defined endpoint. Because there is a specified objective, projects have an endpoint, contrary to the ongoing duties and responsibilities of traditional jobs. Instead of staying in one job, individuals often move from project to project, working with different groups of people. For example, after helping to install a security system, an IT engineer may be assigned to develop a database for a different client.

Third — combined effort of specialists. Unlike much organisational work that is segmented according to functional speciality, projects typically require the combined efforts of a variety of specialists. Instead of working in separate offices under separate managers, project participants — whether engineers, financial analysts, marketing professionals or quality control specialists — work together under the guidance of a project manager to complete a project.

Fourth — nonroutine, with unique elements. This is not an either/or issue but a matter of degree. Accomplishing something that has never been done before — building an electric automobile, or landing two mechanical rovers on Mars — requires solving previously unsolved problems and using breakthrough technology. On the other hand, even basic construction projects that involve established sets of routines and procedures require some degree of customisation that makes them unique.

Fifth — specific time, cost and performance requirements. Projects are evaluated according to accomplishment, cost and time spent. These triple constraints impose a higher degree of accountability than is typically found in most jobs. They also highlight one of the primary functions of project management: balancing the trade-offs among time, cost and performance while ultimately satisfying the customer.

What a project is not

Projects should not be confused with everyday work. A project is not routine, repetitive work. Ordinary daily work typically requires doing the same or similar work over and over, while a project is done only once — a new product or service exists when the project is completed.

Routine, repetitive work Projects
Taking class notes Writing a term paper
Daily entering sales receipts into the accounting ledger Setting up a sales kiosk for a professional accounting meeting
Responding to a supply-chain request Developing a supply-chain information system
Practising scales on the piano Writing a new piano piece
Routine manufacture of a portable music player Designing a player roughly 2 × 4 inches that interfaces with a PC and stores 10,000 songs
Attaching tags on a manufactured product Wire-tag projects for large retailers

Recognising the difference is important because too often resources can be used up on daily operations, which may not contribute to the longer-range organisation strategies that require innovative new products.

Program versus project

In practice the terms project and program cause confusion. They are often used synonymously.

A program is a group of related projects designed to accomplish a common goal over an extended period of time.

Each project within a program has a project manager. The major differences lie in scale and time span. Individual projects within a program are motivated by the higher program goal, and program goals are closely related to broad strategic organisation goals.

LO 1-3 · The project life cycle

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 1 [1].

Another way of illustrating the unique nature of project work is in terms of the project life cycle. The life cycle recognises that projects have a limited lifespan and that there are predictable changes in the level of effort and focus over the life of the project.

There are a number of different life-cycle models in project management literature. Many are unique to a specific industry or type of project — for example, a new-software development project may consist of five phases: definition, design, code, integration/test, and maintenance. A generic cycle has four stages.

1 · Defining stage. Specifications of the project are defined; project objectives are established; teams are formed; major responsibilities are assigned.

2 · Planning stage. The level of effort increases, and plans are developed to determine what the project will entail, when it will be scheduled, whom it will benefit, what quality level should be maintained, and what the budget will be.

3 · Executing stage. A major portion of the project work takes place — both physical and mental. The physical product is produced (e.g., a bridge, a report, a software program). Time, cost and specification measures are used for control. Is the project on schedule, on budget, and meeting specifications? What are the forecasts of each of these measures? What revisions or changes are necessary?

4 · Closing stage. Closing includes three activities: delivering the project product to the customer, redeploying project resources, and conducting a post-project review. Delivery of the project might include customer training and transferring documents. Redeployment usually involves releasing project equipment and materials to other projects and finding new assignments for team members. Post-project reviews include not only assessing performance but also capturing lessons learned.

Defining Planning Executing Closing
Goals Schedules Status reports Train customer
Specifications Budgets Changes Transfer documents
Tasks Resources Quality Release resources
Responsibilities Risks Forecasts Evaluation
Staffing Lessons learned

The project manager

Project managers are often stars in their organisation and well compensated.

They develop, or are given, a project charter or scope statement that defines the objectives and parameters of the project. They work with others to create a project schedule and budget that will guide project execution. They need to understand project priorities so they can make independent decisions.

LO 1-4 · Agile project management

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 1 [1].

Traditional project management focuses on thorough planning up front. Planning requires predictability: for plans to be effective, managers have to have a good understanding of what is to be accomplished and how to do it. When it comes to building a bridge, engineers can draw upon proven technology and design principles to plan and build it.

Not all projects enjoy such predictability.

Project uncertainty

Project uncertainty varies according to the extent the project scope is known and stable, and the extent the technology to be used is known and proven.

However, when the project scope and/or technology is not fully known, things become much less predictable and plan-driven methods suffer.

Such was the case for software development projects, where it was estimated that in 1995 American firms and agencies spent $81 billion on cancelled software projects [2]. Fast forward to 2021, and the cost of poor software quality in the United States was estimated to be over $2 trillion because of unsuccessful projects, software failure, and inferior quality legacy systems [3].

Rolling wave

Fundamentally, Agile PM employs an incremental, iterative process sometimes referred to as a “rolling wave” approach to complete projects.

Instead of trying to plan for everything up front, the scope of the project evolves. The final project design and outcome is not known in great detail and is continuously developed through a series of incremental iterations (waves). Iterations typically last from one to four weeks.

The goal of each iteration is to make tangible progress — defining a key requirement, solving a technical problem, or creating desired features to demonstrate to the customer. At the end of each iteration, progress is reviewed, adjustments are made, and a different iterative cycle begins.

Agile PM focuses on active collaboration between the project team and customer representatives, breaking projects into small functional pieces, and adapting to changing requirements.

It is not simply a question of either/or

Agile methods are often used up front in the defining phase to establish specifications and requirements, and then traditional methods are used to plan, execute and close the project. Agile methods are also used to address certain technical issues on a project while most of the project work is being managed in the traditional way.

Hybrid project management is the name given to this blending of traditional and agile methodologies.

The internal dynamics on Agile projects is quite different from the traditional PM approach. Agile works best in small teams of four to eight members.

Environmental forces driving demand

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 1 [1].

Compression of the product life cycle

One of the most significant driving forces behind the demand for project management is the shortening of the product life cycle.

Today in high-tech industries the product life cycle is averaging six months to three years. Only 30 years ago, life cycles of 10 to 15 years were not uncommon.

Time-to-market for new products with short life cycles has become increasingly important. A common rule of thumb in the world of high-tech product development is that a six-month project delay can result in a 33 percent loss in product revenue share.

Speed, therefore, becomes a competitive advantage; more and more organisations are relying on cross-functional project teams to get new products and services to the market as quickly as possible.

Knowledge explosion

The growth in new knowledge has increased the complexity of projects, because projects encompass the latest advances.

Building a road 30 years ago was a somewhat simple process. Today each area has increased in complexity — including materials, specifications, codes, aesthetics, equipment and required specialists.

Similarly, in today’s digital, electronic age it is becoming hard to find a new product that does not contain at least one microchip. The same is likely to be true for artificial intelligence.

Product complexity has increased the need to integrate divergent technologies. Project management has emerged as the key discipline for achieving this task.

Triple bottom line — planet, people, profit

The threat of global warming has brought sustainable business practices to the forefront. Businesses can no longer simply focus on maximising profit to the detriment of the environment and society.

Efforts to reduce carbon imprints and utilise renewable resources are realised through effective project management. The impact of this movement toward sustainability can be seen in changes in the objectives and techniques used to complete projects — for example, achieving a high LEED certification award is often an objective on construction projects.

Small projects represent big problems

The velocity of change required to remain competitive, or simply keep up, has created an organisational climate in which hundreds of projects are implemented concurrently. This climate has created a multiproject environment and a plethora of new problems. Sharing and prioritising resources across a portfolio of projects is a major challenge for senior management.

Many firms have no idea of the problems involved with the inefficient management of small projects:

  • Small projects typically carry the same or more risk as large projects.
  • Small projects are perceived as having little impact on the bottom line because they do not demand large amounts of scarce resources and/or money.
  • Because so many small projects are going on concurrently, and because the perception of the inefficiency impact is small, measuring inefficiency is usually nonexistent.
  • Many small projects soon add up to large sums of money. Many customers and millions of dollars are lost each year on small projects in product and service organisations.
  • Small projects can represent hidden costs not measured in the accounting system.

Organisations with many small projects going on concurrently face the most difficult project management problems. A key question becomes one of how to create an organisational environment that supports multiproject management. A process is needed to prioritise and develop a portfolio of small projects that supports the mission of the organisation.

In summary, a variety of environmental forces interacting in today’s business world contribute to the increased demand for good project management across all industries and sectors.

LO 1-5 · The technical and sociocultural dimensions

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 1 [1].

Managing projects is a multidimensional process.

The technical dimension

The technical side is the methodology — planning, scheduling and controlling. It is the orderly half of the job, and it produces documents.

A scope statement is what ties the project to the customer, and it is what makes planning and control possible at all: you cannot control against a target nobody wrote down. From it come the deliverables and the work breakdown structure, which is what you actually plan and monitor against.

The WBS is worth thinking of as a database rather than a diagram. It links every level of the organisation to every major deliverable and to every piece of work, down to the individual tasks in a work package. Because those links are built in, a change anywhere is documented, and can be traced back to whatever caused it. That traceability is the whole payoff, and it is why the structure is worth the effort of building properly.

Being well trained in this half is necessary. It is not sufficient.

The sociocultural dimension

Where planning is orderly, implementation is not. It is messy, frequently contradictory, and often paradoxical — and no amount of documentation makes it otherwise.

The manager’s task here is to build a project culture that keeps a team working together and personally invested, and that surfaces problems early enough to do something about them. A culture where bad news travels slowly is a culture that will hand you a surprise late, when it is expensive.

Plans do not survive contact with reality. The skill is not avoiding that; it is recognising the departure quickly and deciding whether to steer back to the plan or change direction.

Underneath all of it is a social network the manager has to build deliberately — across stakeholders who hold genuinely different standards, are committed to different things, and see the same project from incompatible angles. None of that is a failure of the people involved. It is the normal condition of the work.

Master of both

Neither half substitutes for the other, and both have to be actively managed. The practical shape of the job is that you produce a plan and a budget, and at the same time orchestrate the contributions of people you mostly do not control. A manager strong in one half and weak in the other fails — just in a different way each time.

Tuesday — A Day in the Life

Troi manages a large information systems project. She arrives early to get ahead of her team, and immediately meets Akeem, a fellow project manager just back from an overseas project — ten minutes of catching up. At her desk she finds 2 voicemails, 16 emails and 10 posts on her team’s Slack channel from the previous evening; she gives fifteen minutes to her schedule and to-do lists before answering what cannot wait.

Twenty-five minutes on project reports for the standup is interrupted by her manager, who spends twenty minutes discussing the project and shares a rumour about a potential acquisition. The 9:00 a.m. status meeting starts fifteen minutes late because two team members are finishing a job for a client; the remaining forty-five minutes surface issues that have to be assigned for action. Afterwards she spends thirty minutes with Achara, another IS project manager with whom she shares personnel, and offers to free up some of her own team’s time to help Achara’s slipping project.

Downstairs to follow up on a status-report issue, her opening question — “Hi, you all, how are things going?” — draws twenty minutes of complaints, from which she learns that several of the client’s managers are requesting features that were not in the original scope statement. She promises to act on it immediately.

She tries her counterpart John at the client firm and cannot reach him. Eddie from finance drops by for lunch; half an hour in the cafeteria on internal politics, where she is surprised to hear that a powerful ally may be leaving. Back at her desk she reaches John and spends thirty minutes on the problem; he promises to investigate. She sits outside by the creek and meditates for thirty minutes. Then thirty minutes with the purchasing agent, exploring how to get equipment to the site earlier than planned; she authorises express delivery.

A 2:30 Zoom meeting takes ten minutes to get everyone online, then an hour exchanging technical requirements for a new version of a software package. She walks the hallway, thanking Chandra for his analysis at the status meeting. John calls back: the firm’s own marketing rep had made promises about specific features the system would provide. Troi takes the stairs to marketing, waits ten minutes for Mary, and leaves forty minutes later after a heated discussion — having got Mary to agree to straighten out with her own team which commitments were real.

She updates her team on the schedule impact, briefs her manager for twenty minutes, reviews email and project documents for thirty, then spends thirty minutes in MS Project running “what-if” scenarios before writing tomorrow’s reminders and starting her thirty-minute commute home.

How effectively do you think Troi spent her day? What does the case tell you about what it is like to be a project manager?

Editor challenge — attack this claim

“Agile is a response to bad planning. A team that understood its scope properly would not need iterations.”

Attack it. Available lines: the uncertainty figure separates scope from technology, and a team can know one and not the other; iterations buy information that no amount of up-front thinking produces; the $2 trillion figure describes an industry that planned extensively and still failed. But the claim is not entirely empty — decide what part of it survives.

Roommate link

Cooking a dinner party vs. running a restaurant. The dinner party is a project — fixed date, unique menu, guests who will remember it. The restaurant is operations. A chef who runs a restaurant well can still fail at a dinner party, because the failure modes differ: the restaurant’s enemy is variance, the dinner party’s enemy is the thing you forgot to buy.

Now the second half. You cannot cook the dinner party in iterations — the guests arrive once. That is the uncertainty figure, in a kitchen.

Where this sits in the course

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 1 [1].

The next two chapters set the context: how organisations choose which projects to fund (Chapter 2), and the organisational structure and culture a project has to survive inside (Chapter 3).

Then six chapters build the plan. Chapter 4 defines what the project actually covers and decomposes it into a work breakdown structure. Chapter 5 takes on the hardest part — putting credible numbers on cost and time. Chapter 6 turns the WBS into a schedule: activities, sequenced and timed.
