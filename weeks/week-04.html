LO 4-1 · Define the result before planning the work

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 4 [1].

Weeks 2 and 3 settled which project should be done and where it should live. Neither decision tells the team what finished means. Chapter 4 closes that gap.

Project scope describes the result the project will deliver to its customer. A useful scope statement does two jobs at once: it gives the team a boundary for planning, and it gives the customer a basis for acceptance.

The scope is developed with the project manager, customer and other significant stakeholders. It is not a private interpretation written by the team after work has begun.

The scope statement checklist

Element Question it settles
Project objective What will be delivered, by when, for how much and, where relevant, where?
Product scope description What characteristics must the product, service or result have?
Justification Why is the project worth doing?
Deliverables What measurable outputs will exist during and at the end of the project?
Milestones Which important control points will show recognizable progress?
Technical requirements Which specifications, standards or performance levels must be met?
Limits and exclusions What is outside the project, even if someone expects it?
Acceptance criteria What conditions must be satisfied before the customer accepts the result?

The checklist matters because the items are easy to confuse.

  • A deliverable is an output: an approved venue contract, a tested registration system, a finished prototype.
  • A milestone is a control point: venue selected, registration opened, final inspection passed. It has no duration of its own.
  • A technical requirement constrains how well the deliverable must perform.
  • An acceptance criterion is the evidence that lets the customer say yes.
  • A limit or exclusion prevents assumed work from entering the project by silence.

An objective that can govern a plan

A project objective is strongest when one sentence answers four questions:

Deliver what, by when, within what cost, and at what location if location matters.

For the running conference example:

Plan and deliver a two-day manufacturing conference for 500 registered participants in Fargo by 15 October, at a total project cost not exceeding $180,000.

That sentence does not replace the rest of the scope statement. It makes the target visible. The product description still has to state what kind of conference is being promised, the deliverables still have to name the outputs, and the acceptance criteria still have to say how success will be judged.

The 500-person conference

The sponsor asks for “a high-quality regional conference this fall.” The organizing team assumes two days, an evening reception, hybrid streaming and space for 500 people. The sponsor assumes one day, no streaming and a hard ceiling of $120,000. Nobody has written the differences down.

Which entries in the scope checklist would expose the disagreement before contracts are signed? Which entries would still be needed after the disagreement is resolved?

Scope statement, statement of work and charter

Organizations use overlapping labels, so read the document rather than trusting its name.

Scope statement describes the agreed result and its boundaries. In contracted work, a similar document may be called a statement of work (SOW). A project charter performs a different function: it authorizes the project and gives the project manager authority to form the team and begin.

The practical distinction is simple. The charter answers may this project start? The scope statement answers what exactly has been authorized?

LO 4-2 · Scope creep is unmanaged decision-making

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 4 [1].

Scope creep is the gradual expansion of requirements, specifications or priorities after the project begins. A change can improve a project. It becomes creep when its consequences are not deliberately accepted along with it.

Common cause What it looks like
Poor requirements analysis The customer can recognize the desired result but cannot describe it before seeing versions.
Users involved too late The team discovers real operating needs only after design choices have hardened.
Complexity underestimated Unknown interfaces and constraints appear as the work becomes concrete.
Too many decision-makers Compromise adds features because nobody has authority to reject them.
No change control Requests enter the plan without a visible decision on time, cost or performance.
Gold plating The team adds unrequested features because they appear valuable or interesting.

Gold plating is especially deceptive. It feels like craftsmanship, but it spends somebody else’s budget and may make acceptance harder by changing a result the customer did not request.

A controlled change has a path

The essential discipline is that scope and plan change together. Approving a new conference reception without changing budget, schedule, staffing or another deliverable does not remove the trade-off. It hides it.

LO 4-3 · Decide which constraint wins

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 4 [1].

Every project balances time, cost and performance. The chapter’s trade-off model places scope at the performance corner and quality inside the triangle: quality is the result of balancing all three rather than a fourth independent lever.

A priority matrix prevents the team from improvising the ranking every time pressure appears.

Priority Meaning Conference example
Constrain The original target is fixed. The event date cannot move once the venue and speakers are contracted.
Enhance Use opportunities to improve this dimension. Increase program quality or participant value when feasible.
Accept This dimension may move when a trade-off is unavoidable. Spend within an approved contingency to protect the date and program.

The labels are comparative, not aspirational. Marking every criterion “constrain” is a refusal to set priorities; marking every criterion “enhance” is a wish list. The matrix becomes useful only when it tells a project manager what to protect first.

LO 4-4 · The WBS turns scope into manageable work

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 4 [1].

Once the deliverables are known, the project is divided repeatedly into smaller pieces. The result is the work breakdown structure (WBS): a hierarchy in which the complete project is represented by the sum of its lower-level elements.

What a work package must support

The lowest level is a work package. It is not merely the smallest phrase in the diagram. It is the level at which the project can answer five management questions.

Question Work-package field
What work will be completed? Defined result or activity
How long will it take? Duration and dates
What will it cost, and when? Time-phased budget
What resources are required? People, equipment and materials
Who owns it? One responsible person

This is why the WBS becomes a database for the project rather than a picture made at kickoff. Estimates, schedules, budgets, assignments and performance reports all attach to the same codes.

Two tests for decomposition

Coverage. If the child elements do not add up to the parent, something is missing or counted twice. A conference WBS with program, venue and marketing but no registration does not describe the complete project.

Manageability. Stop when the work can be estimated and controlled. Breaking “print badges” into “open the file,” “select print” and “collect pages” creates detail without management value.

Coding the WBS

Numeric indentation records both identity and position:

1       Project
1.1     Major deliverable
1.1.1   Subdeliverable
1.1.1.1 Work package

The code permits costs and progress to be consolidated at any level. Every element beginning 1.2.3 belongs to the same branch, so a system can report that branch alone or roll it into 1.2 and then the project total.

LO 4-5 · The OBS puts an organization behind the work

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 4 [1].

The WBS answers what has to be delivered. The organization breakdown structure (OBS) answers which organizational unit is accountable for delivering it.

The two structures intersect at a cost account: a control point that joins a defined portion of work with the unit responsible for it.

WBS work package Accountable OBS unit Cost account
Confirm keynote speakers Program committee 1.1 / Program
Configure registration Information systems 1.2 / IS
Prepare venue rooms Operations 1.3 / Operations
Launch campaign Marketing 1.4 / Marketing

The intersection matters because a budget without organizational ownership is only a number. Once work and unit are joined, the manager can ask whether the program branch is late, whether the program committee is over budget, or both.

LO 4-6 · Use a PBS when phases organize the work

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 4 [1].

A deliverable-based WBS fits a tangible result whose pieces can be named early. Some projects are defined more naturally by a sequence of phases. Each phase changes the information available to the next.

A process breakdown structure (PBS) organizes work around that evolving process.

The software industry often calls this sequential form a waterfall. The important point is not the label. It is that the hierarchy follows the logic by which the result becomes knowable.

Use a PBS when:

  • the work is primarily a repeatable process;
  • phase completion is the natural management checkpoint;
  • outputs from one phase become inputs to the next; and
  • a component-first decomposition would be artificial or premature.

A project can combine the two structures. Its first level may be phases, with deliverables and work packages beneath each phase. The choice should make ownership, estimating and control clearer.

LO 4-7 · A responsibility matrix is enough for small work

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 4 [1].

Small projects do not always need a separate WBS and OBS. A responsibility matrix places tasks in rows and people in columns, then marks who is responsible and who supports.

Conference task Project manager Program lead Operations lead Marketing lead
Confirm target audience R S S
Contract venue S R
Confirm speakers S R
Open registration S S R
Run event-day check R S S S

R means responsible. S means supports. The matrix is useful only if each task has one unmistakable owner. A row full of support marks and no R describes coordination without accountability.

The matrix is compact, but it does not eliminate the work-package questions. Duration, budget and acceptance still have to live somewhere if the project needs them.

LO 4-8 · Communication is part of the plan

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 4 [1].

A communication plan is not a list of meetings. It is a set of decisions about information.

  1. What information must be collected, and when?
  2. Who needs it?
  3. How will it be gathered, stored and communicated?
  4. Who is permitted to access it?
  5. When and how often will it be sent?
  6. Who provides it?

Match attention to power and interest

The quadrant is a starting point. A regulator may have high power and low daily interest, but the content and timing of required reports are still fixed by regulation. A participant may have little formal power and high interest, making clear general information more useful than executive status meetings.

A communication plan is an operating table

Information Audience Timing Method Provider
Milestone report Sponsor and project manager Biweekly Dashboard and email Project office
Project status and decisions Team and sponsor Weekly Meeting record Project manager
Issue and escalation report Sponsor and affected leads When threshold is crossed Meeting and written record Issue owner
Approved change requests Team, sponsor and affected vendors As approved Change log notification Change authority
Event-day operating update All leads Scheduled checkpoints Shared channel Operations lead

The last column prevents a familiar failure: everybody expects information, but nobody owns producing it. Access belongs in the plan too. Participant records, contract prices and incident details should not be distributed merely because a broad mailing list is convenient.

From definition to control

Chapter 4 is one chain, not eight unrelated tools.

  1. Scope defines the result and its boundary.
  2. Priorities say what wins when the boundary is pressured.
  3. WBS or PBS decomposes the result into controllable work.
  4. OBS or responsibility matrix assigns accountability.
  5. Codes and cost accounts let systems aggregate the plan and actual performance.
  6. Communication plan moves the right information to the people who must act on it.

If any link is missing, the project can still be busy. It cannot be controlled in the same sense.
