LO 5-1 · An estimate is a decision input

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 5 [1].

Week 4 stopped the WBS at work packages that could be estimated, budgeted, resourced and owned. Chapter 5 supplies the numbers. Without credible time and cost estimates, the WBS remains a classification of work rather than a plan.

Estimates support six recurring decisions.

Decision What the estimate provides
Authorization Evidence that the expected benefit justifies the expected time and cost.
Scheduling Durations and effort that allow work to be sequenced and staffed.
Funding Total cost, timing of cash needs and a basis for reserves.
Capacity The people, equipment and materials required by each work package.
Control A baseline against which progress and spending can be compared.
Forecasting A current view of where the project is likely to finish.

An estimate is not a promise that uncertainty has disappeared. It is a structured statement of what the team expects under stated conditions. Its quality depends on whether a decision maker can understand the evidence, the assumptions and the possible outcomes.

The planning horizon changes what can be known

Near-term work usually has better information: the scope is clearer, suppliers can quote, and the people doing the work can inspect the actual conditions. Work farther into the future depends on more assumptions.

The estimate should therefore mature with the project. Early estimates support selection and rough funding. Later estimates should use the WBS, current design, actual productivity and supplier information.

LO 5-2 · Four dimensions describe an estimate

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 5 [1].

Range, accuracy, precision and confidence answer different questions. Treating them as synonyms hides what the estimate actually claims.

Dimension Question Example
Range What outcomes could occur? The work should take 10–15 days.
Accuracy How close was the estimate to the outcome? The final bill was $2,500 after an estimate of $2,500.
Precision How exact is the statement? Fifteen days rather than “by the end of the month.”
Confidence How much trust is placed in the estimate? There is a 90% chance of finishing by Friday.

Range

A range states the set of outcomes considered plausible. “10–15 days” carries more information than “about two weeks” because it exposes the endpoints. A narrow range is useful only when the available evidence justifies it.

For a task with a low estimate of 4 days and a high estimate of 12 days:

\[ \text{range width}=12-4=8\text{ days} \]

The width is not a probability distribution by itself. It is a signal that this task needs discussion, risk analysis or better information.

Accuracy

Accuracy can be judged only after an outcome exists. An estimate of 10–15 days is accurate when the task takes 12 days. If it takes 9 days, the team finished early, but the estimate was still inaccurate because the actual result sat outside the stated range.

That distinction matters for organizational learning. Calling every early finish “good estimating” prevents the estimating system from being calibrated.

Precision

Precision is the degree of exactness. $850 is more precise than “less than $1,000.” 15 days is more precise than “by month-end.” Precision says nothing about whether the number is correct.

A point estimate such as 17.4 days can be highly precise and badly wrong. A range such as 15–22 days can be less precise and much more useful.

Confidence

Confidence states the level of trust placed in the estimate. It is often expressed as a probability, but the probability should be supported by evidence: comparable work, historical error, simulation or informed expert judgment.

“We are 90% confident” is incomplete unless the claim states what is 90% likely: finishing by a date, staying below a cost, or landing inside a range.

The conference registration system

The system lead says configuration will take exactly six days. The vendor has changed the payment interface, no one on the team has used the new version, and the six-day number comes from last year’s installation.

Which parts of the statement concern precision, accuracy and confidence? What range and assumptions would make the estimate more useful?

LO 5-3 · Better estimates come from disciplined conditions

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 5 [1].

Estimate quality is shaped before anyone enters a number in a spreadsheet.

Factors that influence quality

Factor Why it changes the estimate
Planning horizon Distant work contains more unresolved decisions and assumptions.
Project complexity Interactions create nonlinear effort and make omissions more likely.
People Experience, skill, optimism and incentives affect judgment.
Project structure Dedicated teams and clear ownership provide different information from fragmented, part-time work.
Padding Private reserves hide where uncertainty sits and can inflate the total.
Organizational culture A culture that punishes bad news receives optimistic numbers rather than honest ranges.
Other conditions Downtime, holidays, staff availability, currency, inflation and supplier constraints can alter the result.

New technology deserves special attention. Its difficulty does not always grow in a straight line. A small increase in integration or performance requirements can create a much larger increase in testing and coordination.

Seven guidelines

1 · Put responsibility near the work

At the work-package level, the people most familiar with the task should prepare or materially contribute to the estimate. Senior review can challenge assumptions, but authority does not replace task knowledge.

2 · Use more than one informed view

Several estimators can expose missing work and individual bias. Independence matters: a second estimate adds little when it merely repeats the first person’s reasoning.

3 · State normal conditions

An estimate should represent the conditions under which the work is expected to occur. If overtime, a specialist, uninterrupted equipment access or expedited shipping is assumed, say so.

4 · Use consistent units

Hours, days and weeks are not interchangeable labels. Distinguish effort from duration.

  • 40 labor hours describes effort.
  • 5 working days describes elapsed project time under a staffing and calendar assumption.

Two people do not automatically complete forty hours of effort in two and a half days. The work may be indivisible, require coordination or depend on a resource available only part of the day.

5 · Estimate work packages independently

Estimate each WBS task from its own content and conditions before schedule dependencies are applied. Chapter 6 will connect tasks into a network. Combining the steps too early makes it difficult to see whether a long duration comes from the work itself or from waiting for other work.

6 · Keep contingency visible

Do not hide an allowance inside every work package. Private padding destroys the relationship between risk and reserve. Estimate the expected task, assess uncertainty explicitly, and place contingency where it can be seen and governed.

7 · Add risk assessment before commitment

The estimate should name the events and assumptions that could move it. A range without its drivers is difficult to manage; a risk register without cost and schedule effects is disconnected from the baseline.

A useful estimate record contains the scope and WBS code, method, estimator, date, units, low/likely/high values, assumptions, exclusions, risk drivers and the source of any rates or ratios.

LO 5-4 · Method follows the decision

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 5 [1].

No estimating method is best in every condition. The choice depends on the decision, the available detail, the uncertainty and the cost of preparing the estimate.

Condition Top-down tends to fit Bottom-up tends to fit
Strategic decision making ✓
Cost and time are critical commitments ✓
High uncertainty ✓
Internal, small project ✓
Fixed-price contract ✓
Customer requires detail ✓
Unstable scope ✓

The table describes tendencies, not rules. A project can use a top-down estimate for authorization, then replace it with a bottom-up estimate as the WBS becomes stable.

Top-down methods

Top-down estimates begin with the project, a major deliverable or a comparable whole. They are fast and useful before detailed design, but they reveal less about individual work packages.

Consensus

Several knowledgeable people discuss the work and agree on one estimate. The method uses distributed experience, but visible authority and dominant personalities can pull the result toward one view.

Delphi

Experts estimate independently. A facilitator returns the anonymous range and reasoning, and the experts revise through additional rounds. Anonymity reduces pressure to agree with the most senior or forceful person.

Delphi does not remove the need for relevant expertise. A group can reach a stable answer with the same missing assumption.

Ratio

A ratio applies a historical relationship to the new project.

If comparable conferences cost about $180 per attendee, a rough estimate for 300 attendees is:

\[ 300\text{ attendees}\times\$180/\text{attendee}=\$54{,}000 \]

The result is only as comparable as the projects behind the ratio. Venue type, service level, location and inflation can make “cost per attendee” unstable.

Apportion

The apportion method starts with a total project estimate and allocates percentages through the WBS. If historical software projects spend 20% on design, 30% on programming and 40% on testing, those shares provide an early budget structure.

The percentages need a relevant history. Allocating last year’s pattern to a project with a different architecture merely makes an unsupported estimate look structured.

Function points

Software and information-system projects can estimate size through function points. The estimator counts user-facing elements such as inputs, outputs, inquiries, files and interfaces, then applies complexity weights.

Element Low Average High
Inputs 2 3 4
Outputs 3 6 9
Inquiries 2 4 6
Files 4 8 12
Interfaces 5 10 15

The weighted count becomes effort only after it is paired with historical productivity. If five function points normally require one person-month, 500 function points imply about 100 person-months under comparable conditions.

Effort and duration remain different. Adding people does not always divide duration proportionally because the work may be sequential and coordination grows with the team.

Bottom-up methods

Bottom-up estimates begin with work packages and aggregate upward. They cost more to prepare and require stable detail, but they support budgeting, scheduling, resource planning and accountability.

Template method

A completed work package becomes a template for similar work. Reuse is strongest when the record contains scope, conditions and actual results rather than a number alone.

Parametric procedure

A task-level parameter applies a measured rate to a quantity.

\[ 120\text{ screens}\times0.75\text{ labor hours per screen}=90\text{ labor hours} \]

The unit must match the work, and the rate must come from comparable conditions. A blended rate from simple screens should not be applied to a set dominated by complex interfaces.

Range estimate

Range estimating records a low, likely and high value for each work package. The spread directs attention to tasks whose uncertainty can materially affect the plan.

WBS Work package Low Likely High Width Risk driver
1.2.1 Select venue 3 d 5 d 8 d 5 d Contract review
1.3.1 Configure registration 4 d 6 d 10 d 6 d New payment interface
1.4.1 Confirm speakers 6 d 10 d 18 d 12 d Travel and availability

The likely value is not automatically the arithmetic midpoint. It is the outcome considered most plausible under the stated conditions.

Phase estimating is a hybrid

Phase estimating uses a detailed estimate for the next phase and a rough macro estimate for later phases. When the current phase finishes, the next phase receives a detailed estimate and the remainder is revised.

This hybrid fits projects in which later work cannot be specified responsibly until earlier results are known.

LO 5-5 · Cost classification changes what the number means

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 5 [1].

Project costs fall into three broad categories. The label follows the causal relationship and the organization’s cost system.

Category Meaning Examples
Direct cost Can be traced to a specific work package. Task labor, materials, equipment rental, vendor service.
Direct project overhead Supports the project as a whole rather than one work package. Project manager, temporary project office, project-specific systems.
General and administrative overhead Supports the organization across projects and operations. Corporate HR, finance, legal service, executive management, facilities.

The same object can receive different classifications in different organizations. A dedicated conference laptop charged to registration is direct. The standard laptops used by a central IT department across every project are overhead.

Three views of project cost

A cost report can answer three different questions.

  • Scheduled budget: what the baseline planned to spend by this date.
  • Actual cost: what has already been recorded as spent.
  • Committed cost: what the project has obligated through contracts, purchase orders or other commitments, even if the invoice has not arrived.

Looking only at actual cost can create false comfort. A project may appear under budget while signed contracts have already consumed the remaining authorization.

LO 5-6 · Actuals create estimating memory

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 5 [1].

An estimating database stores the connection between what was expected and what happened. It should serve the way work is estimated: by project type, WBS branch, cost code, technology, location or another useful class.

At minimum, preserve:

  • the WBS code and scope of the completed work;
  • the original estimate, range and method;
  • the estimator, date, units and assumptions;
  • actual labor, material, equipment, duration and cost;
  • changes in scope or operating conditions;
  • realized risks and the contingency used; and
  • ratios or productivity measures that can be reused.

A record that says only “registration system: 120 hours” cannot support a new estimate. The next estimator must know what functions were included, who performed the work, which platform was used and what changed during execution.

LO 5-7 · Megaprojects need an outside view

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 5 [1].

Large projects combine long horizons, political commitment, novel interfaces and enormous sunk costs. Their estimates can be distorted by optimism, strategic understatement and the belief that the current project is unlike the failed projects in the historical record.

Reference class forecasting (RCF) counters that inside view with outcomes from comparable completed projects.

Three steps

  1. Select a reference class. Identify completed projects similar enough to inform the current decision: rail tunnels, cargo ships, hospitals or enterprise-system replacements.
  2. Build an outcome distribution. Arrange actual results, such as cost overrun as a percentage of the original estimate, from low to high.
  3. Adjust the forecast. Place the proposed project against that distribution and require evidence before assuming it will outperform the class.

If a proposed tunnel carries a $100 million estimate and comparable regional tunnels averaged a 34% overrun, the outside-view forecast is:

\[ \$100\text{ million}\times1.34=\$134\text{ million} \]

The uplift does not prove that the project will cost exactly $134 million. It changes the burden of proof. Project advocates must explain why the current project should beat the observed base rate.

What the outside view changes

RCF can:

  • reduce optimism and uniqueness bias;
  • make political or strategic understatement harder to ignore;
  • test whether funding is adequate;
  • provide a basis for contingency;
  • improve accountability for the original forecast; and
  • make alternatives comparable on a more realistic basis.

The reference class must be selected carefully. A broad class may hide important differences; a class narrowed until the current project looks unique defeats the method.

LO 5-8 · A white elephant survives its business case

Following Larson & Gray, Ch. 5 [1].

A white elephant is a project or asset whose cost of completion, ownership or operation is out of proportion to the value it delivers, yet which remains difficult to abandon.

Common signals include:

  • demand or benefits have fallen below the original case;
  • operating and maintenance costs require a continuing subsidy;
  • prestige or political commitment replaces evidence;
  • sunk cost is used to justify additional future spending; and
  • no credible exit, repurpose or stop rule exists.

The key decision is forward-looking:

With the evidence available today, would we spend the remaining money to obtain the remaining benefit?

Money already spent may explain how the project arrived at the decision point. It does not by itself justify the next dollar.

The event venue

A city begins a large event venue after forecasting strong regional demand. Construction costs rise, two anchor events withdraw, and the revised operating forecast requires an annual subsidy. Cancelling would make the partially completed structure highly visible.

Which evidence should determine whether the city completes, reduces, repurposes or stops the project? Which parts of the original estimate no longer belong in the decision?

LO A5-1 · Learning changes repeated-task estimates

Following the Chapter 5 appendix in Larson & Gray [1].

When a task is repeated, people and systems often become faster. Setup improves, unnecessary motion disappears and coordination becomes routine. A learning curve estimates that improvement.

One common unit-time form is:

\[ T_n=T_1n^b \]

where:

  • \(T_n\) is the time for the \(n\)th unit;
  • \(T_1\) is the time for the first unit;
  • \(b=\ln(r)/\ln(2)\); and
  • \(r\) is the learning rate, written as a decimal.

With an 80% learning rate, doubling cumulative output reduces the modeled unit time to 80% of its previous level. If the first unit takes 10 hours, the second takes 8 hours and the fourth takes 6.4 hours under this unit-time model.

Learning curves require genuinely repeated work under comparable conditions. They do not justify reducing the estimate for unique design work, and a process or technology change may reset the curve.

State which model is being used. A unit-time curve and a cumulative-average curve use related mathematics but answer different questions.

From scope to a defendable estimate

Chapter 5 joins the WBS to the budget and schedule.

  1. Start with defined work. Estimating a vague scope produces a precise-looking guess.
  2. Choose a method that fits the decision. Early authorization and fixed-price commitment need different evidence.
  3. State range, units and confidence. A point estimate alone hides the claim being made.
  4. Keep assumptions and contingency visible. Hidden padding cannot be governed.
  5. Classify costs consistently. Direct cost, project overhead and G&A answer different questions.
  6. Compare estimate with actual. Completed work improves the next estimate only when the outcome returns to the database.
  7. Use the outside view for large commitments. Reference classes test whether the project is relying on optimism.
