Final Project

A real project, a real partner, an agent you have to govern — 30% of the grade

Teams of four or five run a genuine project-management engagement with an outside organization across the semester. Ten teams, formed by Week 3. Not a case study, not a simulation: a real scope, a real sponsor, a real constraint you did not choose.

Why a partner organization

Everything difficult about this discipline comes from the parts you do not control. A classroom project has a scope you invented, a sponsor who is your instructor, and no cost of being wrong. A partner organization supplies what the exercise otherwise lacks — requirements that shift, a stakeholder whose interest conflicts with yours, and information you have to go and ask for.

The engagement is small on purpose. You are not being asked to deliver the project. You are being asked to manage a defined slice of it and to produce the artifacts that management requires.

Finding a partner

FP0 is due Friday 11 September — do not leave this late

A team without a confirmed partner by that date is assigned one from the instructor’s reserve list, and the reserve projects are less interesting than the ones you find yourselves.

What counts as a partner. Any organization outside this course with a real, bounded piece of work in the next three months: a local manufacturer, a campus unit, a non-profit, a research lab, a student organization with a budget, a startup, a family business. It does not need to be an engineering project.

What you need from them.

  • One named contact willing to spend roughly 30 minutes every other week with your team.
  • A bounded scope with a start and an end inside the semester.
  • Permission to describe the project in a report and a presentation — see confidentiality below.

Where to look. Your own employer or internship; a faculty member’s lab; NDSU facilities, events, or student affairs; the Fargo-Moorhead non-profit sector; a family or friend’s business. Ask early — the request is small and most organizations say yes.

If you cannot find one. Tell the instructor before 11 September rather than after. There is a reserve list. There is no penalty for using it; there is a penalty for silence.

Confidentiality and intellectual property

Some partners will share information they would not want published. Handle it properly:

  • Default to public. If nothing sensitive is involved, your report is a normal academic document.
  • If the partner asks for confidentiality, tell the instructor at FP0. Your report can be marked confidential, read only by the instructor, and excluded from any public archive. Your presentation can be given with figures anonymized.
  • Never sign anything on behalf of NDSU. If a partner asks your team to sign an NDA, stop and contact the instructor. Students may sign personally only after that conversation.
  • The partner owns their data. You own your analysis. Say so in writing, in an email, at the start — a two-sentence email now prevents an ugly conversation in December.

FP3 Risk register with owners and triggers, plus an EVM status report against your baseline Fri 20 Nov 8%
FP4 Final report and presentation 8–11 Dec 10%

Each milestone reuses the method taught the week before it is due. Nothing is asked of you that you have not just practised in studio.

The AI requirement

From FP2 onward, one project artifact must be maintained by an agent you build — the schedule model, the risk register, or the status report. You choose which.

The final report includes a required section, roughly two pages:

  1. What the agent maintained, and its specification — including the acceptance tests you wrote before you built it.
  2. What it got wrong. Every agent that runs for six weeks gets something wrong. A report claiming otherwise is a report whose authors were not checking.
  3. How you caught it — what control surfaced the error, and how long it took.
  4. What you would put in place to govern this agent if the project ran for two years and a sponsor made decisions from its output.
Point 2 is graded generously and point 3 is graded strictly

Finding your agent’s error is not a failure of the work; it is the work. What loses marks is having no mechanism that would have caught it.

Final report structure

Roughly 3,000–4,000 words plus appendices. Sections in whatever order serves the argument, but all present:

  1. The project, the partner, and what success meant to them — on which dimensions, per Week 1.
  2. Delivery approach chosen, and the alternative rejected, with the reason — per Week 12.
  3. Scope: charter and WBS, with what changed and why.
  4. Schedule and cost: the baseline, the actuals, and where they diverged.
  5. Risk: the register, and the risk that materialized or the one you were most wrong about.
  6. The AI section, as above.
  7. What you would do differently, ranked by expected effect rather than by convention.

IMSE 656 students additionally include a methods justification: the choice of delivery approach, estimating method, and control mechanism defended against the empirical literature, with citations to the source shelf.

Presentation

Tuesday 8 and Thursday 10 December. 12 minutes presenting, 5 minutes questions, 3 minutes changeover, enforced. Partner representatives are invited. Details on the Week 16 page.

Rubric

Dimension Weight What full marks look like
Artifact quality 25% WBS passes the 100% rule; the network is correct; the baseline is defensible; the register has owners and triggers, not adjectives.
Evidence and reasoning 25% Choices are defended against a named alternative with a reason, not asserted. Numbers are traceable to a source.
AI governance section 20% A real error found, a real control that caught it, and a governance proposal that would survive a sponsor’s scrutiny.
Partner engagement 15% The partner’s actual constraints are visible in the work. Evidence of regular contact and of scope negotiated rather than assumed.
Communication 15% Report and presentation are readable by the partner, not only by the instructor. Timing respected.

Team working

Ten teams. At 47 students that is seven teams of five and three of four; at 50, ten of five. Teams are formed by Week 3, at FP0.

The five responsibilities

Each carries a duty that cannot be delegated, from the course operations guide:

Role Primary duty Cannot be delegated
Coordinator / Taiga owner Creates the project, manages members, submits URLs Keeping access working and preserving the record
Scope & backlog lead Epics, stories, acceptance criteria, priority Confirming backlog scope matches the approved project
Process facilitator Runs planning, review and retrospective Surfacing blockers and keeping the board truthful
Schedule & resource lead Master schedule file and its version history Protecting the baseline and reconciling changes
Quality, risk & documentation lead Evidence, issues, risk responses, final archive Verifying completion criteria and submission integrity

In a four-person team, merge coordinator with process facilitator — both are keep-the-team-running jobs and the coordinator already owns membership. Scope, schedule and quality each keep a single owner; those three answer for artifacts and cannot be shared.

Roles rotate at FP2 and FP3 so every member drives the master schedule at least once and writes at least one status report.

Every member still plans and performs project work. Each updates their own Taiga tasks weekly and completes at least one documented schedule action — adding dependencies, assigning resources, analysing the critical path, or recording actual progress.

A confidential peer-contribution assessment is collected with FP4 and can adjust an individual’s project grade by up to ±15%. Persistent non-contribution is handled before FP3, not after — tell the instructor while there is still time to fix it.
