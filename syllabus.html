Course description

Project management as it is actually practiced: the quantitative core (work breakdown, critical path method, PERT, earned value management) alongside adaptive delivery (Scrum, Kanban, flow metrics), with hybrid approaches treated as the default real-world case rather than a compromise. The course argues from empirical evidence rather than from convention — students learn to distinguish a base rate from a vendor statistic and to say what a project-success number is actually measuring.

Running through every week is an AI layer. Students progress from grounded prompting, through model-generated artifacts verified by code they write themselves, to specifying and building agents that maintain live project artifacts — and to governing those agents, which is the project-management problem the textbooks have not caught up with yet.

Course objectives

By the end of the course a student can:

  1. Decide which delivery approach fits a given project and defend the choice against a specific alternative.
  2. Build and interpret the core quantitative artifacts: WBS, CPM network, PERT distribution, crash analysis, EVM report.
  3. Run the adaptive artifacts: product backlog, sprint mechanics, cumulative flow diagram, Little’s Law.
  4. Read the evidence critically — distinguish a base rate from a vendor statistic.
  5. Diagnose a failing project and propose interventions ranked by expected effect, not by convention.
  6. Specify, build, and govern an AI agent that maintains a live project artifact, and state what it got wrong.

Additional expectations for students enrolled in IMSE 656

Students enrolled under the graduate number complete the same components at a higher level of expectation:

  • Conceptual assignments include an additional short-answer section requiring students to ground a claim in one of the primary sources on the source shelf.
  • Final project must include a methods justification section: the choice of delivery approach, estimating method, and control mechanism defended against the empirical literature, with citations.
  • Final presentation includes a five-minute defence against a challenge from the instructor drawn from the counter-evidence for the position taken.

Late work

Conceptual assignments close at the posted time and do not reopen; the lowest score is dropped to absorb one missed set. Studio assignments lose 10% per calendar day, to a floor of 50%, for five days, after which they are not accepted. Final project milestones are firm because a partner organization is waiting on them — contact the instructor before the deadline if a milestone is at risk.

Feedback turnaround

Conceptual assignments are graded automatically and available immediately. Studio assignments are returned within seven days. Project milestones are returned within fourteen days.

Attendance

According to NDSU Policy 333, attendance in classes is expected. Students should contact the instructor as soon as possible to notify them of any extended illness or family emergency that might warrant an extension on assignments.

Attendance is not directly a component of the course grade. However, studio assignments are produced in teams during Thursday sessions, and a student who is absent contributes nothing to that deliverable — attendance is therefore consequential in practice even though it is not scored.

Veterans and student service members with special circumstances or who are activated are encouraged to notify the instructor as soon as possible and are encouraged to provide Activation Orders.

Artificial intelligence policy

AI use is expected, not merely permitted. Much of this course is about using these tools well. Three rules make that workable:

  1. Label it. Any submitted artifact that an AI system produced or substantially drafted says so, names the tool, and includes the prompt or a link to the conversation.
  2. Verify it. Every quantitative result generated by a model must be accompanied by the code or hand calculation that checks it. A number you cannot verify is a number you did not produce. This is graded.
  3. Own it. You are responsible for everything you submit, including the parts a model wrote. “The AI said so” is not a defence, in this course or in a project review.

The midterm is the exception: it is closed-book, closed-notes, and computer-free, because the reasoning it tests is the reasoning you need when the tool is unavailable or wrong.

What this course deliberately does not cover

Stating exclusions explicitly is more useful than pretending to comprehensiveness:

  • PMP exam drilling. The exam changes; the reasoning does not. The credential is mentioned, not taught to.
  • Tool tutorials (MS Project, Jira, Primavera). One is assigned as homework infrastructure; no lecture time goes to menus.
  • The full process catalogue of PMBOK 8. You learn the shape of a process standard and how to look things up. Memorizing inputs-tools-outputs is the lowest-value activity in project-management education.
  • Scaling frameworks in depth (SAFe, LeSS, Nexus). One segment on why they exist and why the evidence for them is thin.
  • Six Sigma and lean manufacturing. Adjacent field, separate course.
  • Contract law. Contract types and incentive structure are covered; the law is not.
