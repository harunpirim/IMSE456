AI Studio

Gemini, Antigravity, the source shelf, and the study loop

Project management is one of the first disciplines where the routine artifacts — charters, work breakdowns, risk registers, status reports — can be drafted by a machine in seconds. That does not make the discipline easier. It moves the difficulty from production to judgement: someone still has to know whether the WBS is complete, whether the estimate is defensible, and whether the status report is telling the truth.

This course teaches that judgement by making you use the tools constantly and hold them to account every time.

Build → Use → Reconcile

Every Thursday studio runs the same three movements, and the AI sits inside the first one. You build the tool — often by directing an agent to write it — then use the professional software on the same data, then explain where the two disagree.

This is the entire argument for teaching project management to engineers rather than leaving it to a certification. A course that only teaches ProjectLibre produces students who can operate a tool until the version changes. A course that only teaches the mathematics produces students who have never seen a resource histogram. Building it and then using it produces the one capability that transfers: knowing when the tool is wrong.

The study loop

The five roles

Five roles, five different jobs. The framework is Sandeep Swadia’s “University in a Box” from How To Become Dangerously Self-Educated (with AI). None of it is graded — it is the fastest way to find out what you have wrong before an assignment does it for you.

Role Its job Where you meet it
Adviser Sets destination, baseline, sequencing The syllabus and the schedule
Librarian Curates credible sources, defends against distraction The source shelf below, and per-week readings
Tutor Asks one question at a time, finds the gap, does not lecture “Tutor check” in every week’s notes; the per-week prompt at the foot of each lab sheet
Editor Challenges logic, finds weak arguments, cuts repetition “Editor challenge” — a defensible-sounding claim you have to attack
Roommate Brings perspective from an unrelated field “Roommate link” — a cross-domain analogy that transfers structure, not vocabulary

Tutor prompt

Be my tutor on [TOPIC] in project management. Ask me one question at a
time. Don't lecture me. Find the gap in my understanding. Be precise.

When I get something wrong, don't correct me immediately — ask a follow-up
that makes me find the error myself. Only explain after I've tried twice.

Two commands I'll use: "teach me" means give me the explanation now; "test
me" means switch to harder questions on the same topic.

For exam preparation, the retrieval variant — mixed questions drawn from all prior weeks, not the current one. Mixing is what makes retrieval practice work.

Editor prompt

Attack what I just wrote. Find the weakest link in the argument, the claim
that isn't supported, and the place where I've confused a definition with
a conclusion. Be specific and be harsh. Don't tell me what's good about it.

Roommate prompt

I've just learned [CONCEPT] in project management. You work in a completely
different field. Where does this same structure show up in your world, and
what does your version know that mine doesn't?

The source shelf

Everything else is optional reading. Load these into a single grounded workspace and make the assistant answer from the shelf, not from the open web.

Standards — look at them; do not memorize them.

  • PMI, A Guide to the Project Management Body of Knowledge (PMBOK Guide) — Eighth Edition, November 2025 — https://www.pmi.org/standards/pmbok
  • Schwaber & Sutherland, The Scrum Guide, November 2020 — https://scrumguides.org
  • Beck et al., Manifesto for Agile Software Development, 2001 — https://agilemanifesto.org
  • ISO 21502:2020, Project, programme and portfolio management — Guidance on project management

Empirical anchors — the papers this course actually argues from.

  • Gemino, Horner Reich & Serrador (2021), “Agile, Traditional, and Hybrid Approaches to Project Success”, Project Management Journal. 477 projects.
  • Serrador & Pinto (2015), “Does Agile work? — A quantitative analysis of agile project success”, IJPM. 1,002 projects.
  • Koch et al. (2023), “The affective, behavioural and cognitive outcomes of agile project management: A preliminary meta-analysis”, JOOP. Pre-registered; k = 41, N = 73,825.
  • Young & Jordan (2008), “Top management support: mantra or necessity?”; Young & Poon (2013), IJPM.
  • Batselier & Vanhoucke (2015), “Empirical Evaluation of Earned Value Management Forecasting Accuracy”, JCEM. 51 real projects.
  • Flyvbjerg (2021), “Top Ten Behavioral Biases in Project Management”, PMJ. Base rates from 2,062 projects.
  • Love, Ika et al. (2019), “The cost performance of transportation projects: The fallacy of the Planning Fallacy account”, TR Part A. The counter-case.
  • Eveleens & Verhoef (2010), “The Rise and Fall of the Chaos Report Figures”, IEEE Software 27(1):30–36.
The two failure modes

The model agrees with you. Every prompt above is written to make the assistant adversarial, because the default behaviour is accommodation. If your Editor pass comes back mostly positive, the prompt didn’t take — re-run it with “you were too generous; find three more problems and be harsher.”

The model invents a citation. Ask for a source and you will often get a plausible-looking one that does not exist, or a real paper with the wrong finding attached. Anything you are going to put in a report, verify against the actual document. This is the entire reason the Librarian is a separate step rather than something you do inline.

The weekly cycle

Roughly 60–90 minutes per chapter, outside class. Not graded.

1. TUTOR (diagnostic)  — 10 min — what do I already wrongly believe?
2. Read the module     — 20 min — notes + one primary source
3. TUTOR (voice)       — 20 min — one question at a time, no lecturing
4. Write something     — 15 min — a plan, an argument, a worked problem
5. EDITOR              — 10 min — attack what I wrote
6. ROOMMATE            —  5 min — where else does this structure appear?
7. TUTOR (retrieval)   —  5 min — mixed questions from all prior weeks

Steps 4 and 5 do the work. The Tutor finds gaps; producing something and having it attacked is what closes them. A loop that stops after step 3 feels productive and mostly isn’t.
